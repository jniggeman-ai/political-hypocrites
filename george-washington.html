President Series: Part I

George WashingtonThe Man Who Invented the Presidency

A first-person deep dive into what he was for, what he accomplished, what he was criticized for, and whether America would still see him the way we're taught to today.

By Julian
Published August 2026
Sources Founders Online, Mount Vernon, Chernow, Ellis, National Archives, NPS
Share on X

Every other president in American history had a template to work from. Washington didn't. This is the first entry in the President Series: first-person deep dives into every American president, in order, covering what they were for, what they accomplished, what they were criticized for, and how their presidency and personality would be viewed by Americans today.

Sourcing standard: Every factual claim in this piece is sourced, drawing primarily on the National Archives' "Founders Online" collection of Washington's correspondence, George Washington's Mount Vernon, Ron Chernow's Washington: A Life, Joseph Ellis's His Excellency: George Washington, and National Park Service historical materials. Opinion is marked inline wherever it appears and is never blended silently into the factual record. This draft has been through external fact-check review.

Part One

Introduction

I'm starting the President Series with George Washington, and I want to be upfront about why. Every other president in American history had a template to work from. Washington didn't. He wasn't just the first man to hold the office, he was the man who decided what the office would even look like, and he built it with almost no roadmap, no precedent, and no guarantee it would survive contact with reality.

Opinion I think that makes him one of the most consequential human beings in the last five hundred years, not just American history. He had the most powerful army in the young country's history at his personal command after the Revolutionary War, and instead of using it to become a king, which is what nearly every successful revolutionary general in human history had done before him, he handed it back to a civilian government and went home to his farm. King George III reportedly said that if Washington actually did that, he would be the greatest man in the world. He did it. Twice.

This series is going to hold every president to the same standard, and that means telling the full story, not a flattering one and not an unforgiving one. Washington's presidency built the institutions that let the United States become, in my view, the most successful experiment in self-government in human history. Opinion He turned a fragile coalition of thirteen suspicious former colonies into a functioning federal republic, established the precedent of a peaceful transfer of power, and set a standard of restraint in office that most world leaders before or since never came close to matching. That accomplishment is enormous, and I'm not going to undersell it just because it's more comfortable to focus on his failures.

He also owned human beings, and his administration's policy toward Native nations resulted in broken treaties and forced displacement. Those facts are real, they're documented, and I'm going to describe them with the same directness I use for everything he got right. Opinion I don't think acknowledging that requires tearing down what he built. Two things can be true. He was a man of his era who participated in institutions that era considered normal and this country would later abolish, and he was also the architect of a system of government that has outlasted nearly every other one on earth. I'm not interested in flattening either half of that to make him easier to categorize.

What I want this piece to do is put you inside his presidency the way people actually experienced it, the achievements and the controversies both, without the benefit of two hundred years of hindsight telling you how the story ends. I'll cover the man before the presidency, because it explains a lot about the decisions he made once he had the job. I'll cover what he built, in real detail. I'll cover what he got wrong, in equally real detail. And I'll end by asking a question I think is worth sitting with: if George Washington ran for president today, in the current media environment, would the country still see him the way we're taught to see him now?

Part Two

Formation of the Man

George Washington was not born into the world he'd eventually help create. He was born on February 22, 1732, on a farm in Westmoreland County, Virginia, into a family that was comfortable but not elite by Virginia planter standards. Opinion I think that detail matters more than most biographies give it credit for. Washington spent his entire life around men who had more money, more formal education, and more social polish than he did, and it shaped how hard he worked to prove himself.

His father died when he was eleven, which cut off any chance of the formal English education his older half-brothers had received. Washington's schooling stopped at roughly the equivalent of a grade school level. What he had instead was mathematics, surveying, and an intense, almost compulsive attention to self-improvement. As a teenager he copied out by hand a set of etiquette guidelines called the Rules of Civility and Decent Behavior, more than a hundred rules on how a gentleman was supposed to carry himself in public. Opinion That's not a small detail either. He was, from a young age, consciously constructing the persona he wanted the world to see, long before anyone outside Virginia knew his name.

At sixteen, Washington got work as a surveyor on the Virginia frontier, mapping land for the wealthy Fairfax family. It exposed him early to two things that would define his career: the physical hardship of frontier life, and the enormous, half-mapped territory to the west that everyone in colonial Virginia understood would eventually be worth a fortune. By his early twenties he had used his surveying income to start acquiring land of his own, a habit he never really stopped.

His first real brush with history came in 1754, at twenty two years old, when he was sent by the governor of Virginia into the Ohio Valley to confront French forces encroaching on territory Britain claimed. What followed was, by any honest account, a disaster he was directly responsible for. Washington's small force ambushed a French diplomatic party at a place called Jumonville Glen, killing its commander under circumstances that are still disputed. The French response came swiftly. Washington was forced to surrender a poorly built, poorly positioned fort he'd named, without irony, Fort Necessity. He signed a surrender document, written in French, a language he did not read, that included language admitting to the assassination of the French officer. Opinion This is not a footnote historians invented to embarrass him. It is widely credited as one of the sparks of the French and Indian War, a conflict that eventually became the global Seven Years' War. A twenty two year old Virginia militia officer's mistake helped ignite a war that reshaped the map of North America.

He didn't quit after that failure, and that matters. Washington spent the next several years as an aide to British General Edward Braddock, and in 1755 survived Braddock's catastrophic defeat near the Monongahela River, an ambush that killed Braddock and devastated his force. Washington had two horses shot out from under him and four bullet holes through his coat. He walked away physically unharmed and organized the retreat himself. Opinion I think this is where the legend genuinely starts, not with cherry trees, but with a young man who had already failed publicly once, then rode directly into the deadliest battle of his career and came out of it looking like he couldn't be killed. Word of his conduct spread through the colonies. He was twenty three.

By the time he resigned his militia commission in 1758, still frustrated that the British army refused to give provincial officers like him equal rank and respect, Washington had already lived through military disaster, personal humiliation, and a battlefield near-death experience that made him locally famous. He married Martha Dandridge Custis in 1759, a wealthy widow whose fortune substantially expanded his own, and settled in at Mount Vernon as a Virginia planter and member of the House of Burgesses. Opinion For the next fifteen years he looked, from the outside, like a man whose adventurous years were behind him. They weren't. They were the training ground for everything that came next.

Part Three

Revolutionary War Commander

By 1774, Washington was no longer just a Virginia planter. He'd spent years watching British policy tighten around the colonies, and he attended both the First and Second Continental Congress as a Virginia delegate, showing up in his old militia uniform, a deliberate signal that he was prepared to fight. When the Second Continental Congress needed someone to command the newly formed Continental Army in June 1775, Washington was the near unanimous choice. Opinion Part of that was merit. Part of it, and I think this deserves to be said plainly, was politics. Congress needed a Southerner to lead an army fighting outside Boston, to make the rebellion look like a united colonial project rather than a New England quarrel. Washington was available, respected, and Virginian. He accepted the commission and refused a salary, asking only to be reimbursed for expenses.

What followed was not the story of a natural born military genius running the table against the world's most powerful army. It was closer to the opposite. Washington lost more battles than he won. He was outmaneuvered and driven out of New York in 1776 in a string of defeats that nearly ended the war before it really started. He lost Philadelphia, the seat of the Continental Congress, in 1777. Opinion If you judge Washington purely as a battlefield tactician, the honest record is mixed at best. He was frequently out-generaled by British commanders with more formal training and better resourced armies, and he knew it.

What he was exceptional at was something less flashy and, in my view, far more important for winning the actual war: he understood that as long as the Continental Army existed as a functioning force, the rebellion was alive, and the British could not fully claim victory. That strategic patience is why the crossing of the Delaware on Christmas night, 1776, followed by the surprise victories at Trenton and Princeton, mattered so much. The Continental Army was in shambles, enlistments were about to expire, and Washington needed a win to keep the cause from collapsing entirely. He got one, at real risk, in brutal winter conditions, against Hessian forces that badly underestimated him.

Valley Forge, during the winter of 1777 to 1778, is the moment most Americans associate with the war's hardship, and the historical record backs that reputation up. Roughly 2,000 of the 12,000 soldiers encamped there died over the winter from disease, malnutrition, and exposure.[1] Opinion What I think gets lost in the popular image of Valley Forge as pure suffering is that it was also where the Continental Army became a real army. Washington brought in Baron Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben, a Prussian officer, to drill the troops in European-style discipline and tactics. The force that marched out of Valley Forge in the spring of 1778 was a fundamentally more capable fighting force than the one that had marched in.

Washington also had to survive an attempt by members of his own officer corps and allies in Congress to have him replaced, an episode historians call the Conway Cabal, named for General Thomas Conway, one of its most vocal figures. The details of how organized or serious the plot actually was are still debated among historians, but the basic fact isn't: there was real, documented dissatisfaction with Washington's leadership after the losses of 1776 and 1777, and serious people in positions of power discussed replacing him. Opinion He survived it through political skill as much as battlefield success, cultivating relationships in Congress and among his officers that outlasted the whisper campaign against him. It's a detail I think is important precisely because it cuts against the myth of Washington as a figure everyone simply deferred to by default. He had to fight for his position, more than once, against his own side.

By the time the war shifted decisively south and then to Yorktown in 1781, Washington's real skill had become undeniable: not tactical brilliance in any individual battle, but the ability to hold a fragile, underfed, underpaid army together through years of losses, and to keep it in the field long enough for French naval and military support to tip the balance. The joint American and French operation at Yorktown, trapping Cornwallis's army against the coast with the French fleet cutting off any escape by sea, ended in a British surrender in October 1781 and effectively ended the war.

Part Four

The Moment He Became a Legend

The war was effectively over after Yorktown, but Washington's most consequential decision as a military commander was still ahead of him, and it had nothing to do with a battlefield.

By early 1783, the Continental Army was sitting largely idle, and its officers were furious. Congress had promised pensions and back pay that it had no reliable way to fund under the weak Articles of Confederation. In March 1783, at the army's encampment in Newburgh, New York, anonymous letters began circulating among the officer corps, calling for a meeting to discuss what amounted to a threat: either march on Congress and force payment, or refuse to disband once the war officially ended. Opinion This is, in plain terms, a coup being seriously discussed by the armed force that had just won the country its independence. That's not a dramatic overstatement. It's what the documentary record shows.

Washington heard about the meeting and showed up uninvited. He hadn't planned to speak at length. He addressed the officers directly, arguing that any action against Congress would undo everything the war had accomplished. Partway through, he paused to read a letter from a congressman, and struggled to make out the words. He reached into his coat for a pair of reading glasses that almost none of his officers had ever seen him wear in public, and said, according to accounts written down afterward by men who were in the room, that he had grown gray in the service of his country and now found himself growing blind as well.[2] The room reportedly went silent, and several hardened officers wept. The threat of a military coup dissolved that day. Opinion I don't think it's an exaggeration to say that single, small, human gesture, an aging general admitting he needed glasses, may have done more to secure the future of American democracy than any battle he fought.

The larger act came several months later. With the war formally concluded by the Treaty of Paris in September 1783, Washington traveled to Annapolis, Maryland, where Congress was then sitting, and on December 23, 1783, formally resigned his commission as commander in chief, handing control of the military back to the civilian government he served. At that point in history, this was close to unprecedented. Successful revolutionary generals, going back through recorded history, did not typically hand back power voluntarily. They used the armies that had won them their wars to seize political control. Washington had, by most accounts, the personal loyalty of his army and the standing to have made himself a monarch or a military dictator with minimal resistance. Opinion He chose not to, and I think that decision is one of the single most important acts in the entire history of American self-government, arguably more important than any policy he enacted as president. It set the precedent, immediately and unmistakably, that in America military power answers to civilian authority.

King George III, when told that Washington intended to give up power and return to his farm, reportedly told the American-born painter Benjamin West that if Washington actually did that, he would be the greatest man in the world.[3] Washington did it. Thomas Jefferson, watching from Paris, called it an act that "excited the astonishment of the world," noting how unusual it was for anyone with Washington's power to voluntarily lay it down.[4]

Washington returned to Mount Vernon in late 1783 expecting, by his own account, to live out his remaining years as a private farmer. Opinion He was wrong about that, but the fact that he genuinely believed his public life was over, and was still willing to leave power on the table when he believed it, is exactly why the country trusted him enough to hand him that power again a few years later, this time as president.

Part Five

The Constitutional Convention

Washington's retirement at Mount Vernon lasted about four years, and it was not the peaceful farming life he'd told Jefferson he wanted. The country he'd helped win independence for was, by 1786 and 1787, showing serious signs of coming apart. The Articles of Confederation, the loose framework governing the states since 1781, gave the national government almost no real power. Congress couldn't levy taxes, couldn't regulate trade between states, and couldn't field an army without asking the states nicely and hoping they complied.

Shays' Rebellion, an uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers in 1786 and early 1787 who shut down courts and threatened a federal armory to stop foreclosures, alarmed Washington badly. Opinion He wrote to friends that the events in Massachusetts filled him with the deepest concern he'd felt for the country since the war itself, worse in some ways, because it suggested the American experiment might collapse from the inside rather than being defeated by an outside power. That fear is a big part of why he agreed to attend the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia in the summer of 1787, despite having told people, repeatedly and apparently sincerely, that he was done with public life.

Washington's actual contribution to the Constitutional Convention as a drafter of specific policy was limited. He spoke on the floor only once during the entire summer, on the convention's final day, to support a minor change to the ratio used for congressional representation.[5] Opinion But I think measuring his importance by word count badly misunderstands why he mattered there. Washington was elected the convention's president on its first day, unanimously, and his mere presence in the room did something no argument or clause could do on its own: it told the country and the world that this gathering was legitimate.

Other delegates, men like James Madison and Alexander Hamilton, did the intellectual heavy lifting on the actual structure of the document. Washington's role was closer to that of a guarantor. People trusted that whatever emerged from that room, if Washington had presided over it and put his name behind it, was serious and not some backroom power grab.

His presence also shaped the convention in a more specific way. Several delegates have documented that the office of the presidency, as eventually written into Article II, was designed with real confidence because everyone in the room assumed Washington would be the first person to hold it.[6] Opinion That is an unusual thing to say about any government position in history. The framers gave the executive branch more independent authority than many delegates were initially comfortable with, in part because they trusted the specific man they expected to occupy the office first. It's a reminder that the Constitution wasn't drafted in a vacuum of abstract political theory. It was drafted by people making calculated bets on how much power a known, trusted individual could be handed safely.

When the Convention finished its work in September 1787, Washington signed the document and it was sent to the states for ratification, a fight that would consume the following year. Opinion He didn't campaign hard for ratification in public the way Hamilton and Madison did through the Federalist Papers, but his known support for the document carried enormous weight in state ratifying conventions, particularly in Virginia, where the vote was close and where his personal credibility likely tipped wavering delegates.

By early 1789, with the Constitution ratified and the first federal elections underway, there was essentially no debate about who the first president would be. The Electoral College cast its votes in February 1789, and Washington received every single vote from every elector, the only president in American history to be elected unanimously.[7] Opinion He hadn't sought the office. He'd made a public habit of saying he didn't want it. And the country handed it to him anyway, unanimously, because it trusted almost no one else with the job of inventing what the presidency would actually be.

Part Six

Inventing the Presidency

Washington took the oath of office on April 30, 1789, on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York City, then serving as the nation's temporary capital. He reportedly added the words "so help me God" to the oath prescribed by the Constitution, a tradition not required by the document itself and first documented decades after the fact, but repeated by nearly every president since.[8] Opinion I think it's worth pausing on just how strange his position was that day. He wasn't inheriting an office with rules, customs, or precedent. He was the precedent. Every choice he made, how to dress, what to call himself, how often to speak publicly, how the government would physically run day to day, was going to become the default for the job forever after, whether he intended that or not.

Some of what he decided was almost trivial on its face but became permanent anyway. There was a real debate in the earliest days of his presidency, driven mostly by Vice President John Adams and a Senate committee, over what to formally call the president. Suggestions included "His Excellency," "His Highness," and even "His Highness the President of the United States and Protector of their Liberties."[9] Washington was reportedly uncomfortable with the more monarchical suggestions, and the House of Representatives, led by James Madison, pushed back hard against them. The country settled on the plain "Mr. President." Opinion That might sound like a footnote, but I think it says something real about the kind of republic the country was trying to build in its very first weeks, and Washington's discomfort with grander titles helped steer it in that direction.

The two term precedent is the decision most people know Washington for, and it deserves the credit it gets. Nothing in the Constitution as originally written limited how many terms a president could serve. Washington could very plausibly have been reelected for the rest of his life. Instead, after two terms, he stepped down voluntarily in 1797, establishing an unwritten rule that held for a century and a half until Franklin Roosevelt broke it in 1940, after which the country formally wrote Washington's precedent into the Constitution as the 22nd Amendment in 1951.[10] Opinion I'd put this alongside his resignation of his military commission as one of the two most important decisions of his public life. He had the standing to hold onto power indefinitely, in a young country that had no real mechanism to stop him if he'd chosen to, and he walked away anyway, on purpose, to prove the office was bigger than the man holding it.

Washington also built the practical architecture of the executive branch essentially from scratch. The Constitution mentions departments only in passing, so Washington and Congress had to decide what the government would actually look like in practice. He created the first cabinet, appointing Thomas Jefferson as Secretary of State, Alexander Hamilton as Secretary of the Treasury, Henry Knox as Secretary of War, and Edmund Randolph as Attorney General.[11] Opinion The Constitution doesn't require a cabinet or describe how one should function. Washington invented the practice of gathering his top officials for regular consultation, and every president since has followed some version of the system he built out of necessity, not because the founding document told him to.

He also established, through repeated practice rather than explicit constitutional instruction, the working relationship between the presidency and the other two branches. He generally respected Senate confirmation of appointments and treaties, though he grew frustrated with the process quickly, memorably storming out of one 1789 Senate session over what he felt was excessive interference in treaty negotiations with Southern Native nations, reportedly saying he would "be damned if he ever went there again," and largely stopped seeking the Senate's advice in person after that episode.[12] Opinion I think that moment is a useful corrective to the idea that Washington glided through the invention of the presidency with total serenity. He was frequently improvising under real institutional friction, testing what the other branches would tolerate, and adjusting when they pushed back.

By the end of his first term, the presidency had gone from a page and a half of constitutional text to a functioning office with a cabinet, a set of diplomatic and ceremonial customs, an established relationship with Congress, and a strong expectation that whoever held it would eventually give it up. Opinion None of that was inevitable. A different first president, a more ambitious or less disciplined one, could have set the office on a very different trajectory. It's a large part of why I think Washington's presidency deserves to be judged as much by the institution he built as by any individual policy that came out of it.

Part Seven

Domestic Accomplishments

If Washington's first term was about inventing the shape of the presidency, a large part of his actual governing agenda was about solving a problem that could have ended the country before it really started: the United States was effectively bankrupt.

The national government had emerged from the Revolutionary War carrying roughly $54 million in debt, and the states carried a further $25 million between them, obligations owed to foreign governments, domestic bondholders, and unpaid soldiers who had fought the war on the promise of eventual compensation.[13] Alexander Hamilton, as Washington's Treasury Secretary, proposed a plan to address this that became one of the most consequential and controversial economic programs in early American history. Opinion I think it's important to say plainly that this was fundamentally Hamilton's intellectual work. Washington's role was different, and in its own way just as important: he was the one who had to decide whether to back it, absorb the political cost of doing so, and hold the government together while it tore his cabinet apart internally.

Hamilton's plan had three major components. First, the federal government would assume the remaining state war debts, folding them into a single national obligation. Second, that debt would be funded at full face value rather than discounted, meaning the government would honor its original bonds completely, a decision that rewarded speculators who had bought up war bonds cheaply from struggling veterans and original holders, and that fact was not lost on critics at the time. Third, Hamilton proposed a national bank, modeled loosely on the Bank of England, to manage the government's finances and stabilize the young nation's currency and credit.[14]

Thomas Jefferson and James Madison opposed the plan bitterly, particularly the assumption of state debts, which primarily benefited Northern states like Massachusetts that had large outstanding war debts, over Southern states like Virginia that had already paid theirs down. Opinion This fight is, in my view, the real starting point for the sectional and ideological divide that would define American politics for the next century. It wasn't an abstract disagreement. It was North versus South, financial centers versus agrarian interests, dressed up as a debate over constitutional interpretation.

Washington backed a resolution that has become one of the most famous political trades in American history, though the popular version of the story is neater than the actual record supports. Around June 20, 1790, Jefferson hosted a private dinner at his lodgings where he, Madison, and Hamilton discussed what's generally called the Compromise of 1790: Madison would stop actively fighting the assumption plan in Congress, in exchange for Hamilton's support in placing the permanent national capital on the Potomac River, in what would become Washington, D.C.[15] The only firsthand account of that dinner is Jefferson's own, and it was written down a year or more after the fact, not in the days immediately following. Historians who have examined the surrounding record note that negotiations over both the capital's eventual location and the size of Virginia's debt burden had been underway for weeks before the three men sat down to eat, and that Pennsylvania's support for a ten-year interim capital in Philadelphia was already largely secured by that point. What the dinner most plausibly did was help finalize a bargain that was already taking shape, including a further concession, not mentioned in Jefferson's own account of the evening, that reduced Virginia's share of the assumed debt by roughly $1.5 million. Opinion Washington himself did not attend that dinner, but he was Virginia's most trusted figure and the broader package required his approval to move forward. His willingness to back a compromise that gave multiple factions something real, rather than letting the fight fester and potentially break apart the union in its first years, is a form of leadership that gets far less attention than his military record but mattered just as much to the country's survival, even if the single-dinner version of the story oversimplifies how that compromise actually came together.

The debate over the national bank raised a deeper constitutional question that Washington personally had to resolve. Jefferson argued the Constitution nowhere gave the federal government explicit authority to charter a bank, and that creating one exceeded the government's proper powers. Hamilton countered with what became known as the doctrine of implied powers, arguing the Constitution's "necessary and proper" clause gave Congress authority to take reasonable action in pursuit of its enumerated powers, even without explicit textual permission.[16] Washington, after formally requesting written opinions from both men, sided with Hamilton and signed the bank bill into law in February 1791. Opinion That single decision set a precedent for how the federal government would interpret its own constitutional authority that has echoed through American law and politics ever since, well beyond the specific question of a bank.

Washington's domestic record during this period isn't only about Hamilton's financial program. His administration also saw the ratification of the Bill of Rights in December 1791, the first ten amendments to the Constitution, originally proposed by Madison in response to widespread concern, including from Washington himself, that the original document didn't do enough to explicitly protect individual liberties.[17] Opinion Washington wasn't the primary author of the Bill of Rights, but he supported its passage and signed the legislation that sent it to the states, and I think that's worth stating clearly, because it undercuts any modern reading of him as indifferent to individual rights. He governed a country that, in its first three years under his leadership, stabilized its finances, established federal credit that would hold for generations, resolved a serious constitutional dispute over the scope of federal power, and enshrined explicit protections for individual liberty into its founding law. That's a genuinely remarkable governing record for any presidency, let alone the first one, built with no template to work from.

Part Eight

The Whiskey Rebellion

Hamilton's financial program needed revenue to actually work, and in 1791, at his recommendation, Congress passed an excise tax on distilled spirits, the first tax the federal government ever levied directly on a domestic product.[18] On paper it looked like a straightforward way to help pay down the war debt the government had just assumed. In practice, it landed hardest on small farmers in the western frontier regions of Pennsylvania, Virginia, and other states, for whom distilling surplus grain into whiskey wasn't a luxury business but a practical necessity. Grain was heavy, expensive, and difficult to transport over the Appalachian mountains to eastern markets. Whiskey was compact, durable, and often functioned as an informal currency on the frontier.[19] Opinion The tax fell disproportionately on people who had the least cash on hand to pay it, and that's not a partisan reading, it's simply how the economics worked.

Resistance built for three years. Federal tax collectors in western Pennsylvania were tarred and feathered, threatened, and in some cases physically attacked. By the summer of 1794, the situation escalated sharply. A federal marshal serving court papers near Pittsburgh was met by an armed crowd, and shortly after, several hundred armed men attacked the home of a regional tax inspector, burning it to the ground. Estimates of the crowd that later gathered outside Pittsburgh to discuss further action range as high as 7,000 people, in a region with a total population far smaller than that number suggests real, organized, armed resistance to federal authority.[20]

Washington's response is one of the more consequential and, in my view, underappreciated decisions of his presidency. He invoked the Militia Act of 1792 and called up roughly 13,000 militia troops from Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Maryland, and Virginia, a force larger than the army he had commanded at multiple points during the Revolutionary War itself.[21] Opinion I want to be direct about what that means. The first president of the United States personally led federal troops, at least for part of the march, against American citizens on American soil, to enforce a federal law those citizens were refusing to comply with. That is a real and significant use of centralized federal power against the country's own people, and it deserves to be described in exactly those terms rather than softened into something more comfortable.

At the same time, the way Washington used that force matters to understanding the episode fully. He did not seek criminal prosecutions on a mass scale, and he did not use the militia to conduct a punitive campaign against the region. The show of overwhelming force was largely enough. Resistance collapsed without a significant battle by the time the militia arrived in western Pennsylvania in the fall of 1794. Of the roughly twenty men eventually arrested and tried for treason, only two were convicted, and Washington personally pardoned both of them in 1795.[22] Opinion I think that combination, an overwhelming and unmistakable assertion of federal authority, followed by clemency once the point had been made, is genuinely characteristic of how Washington operated. He was willing to use real force to establish that federal law would be enforced, but he wasn't interested in using that moment to crush or humiliate the people who had resisted it.

The episode set a precedent that outlasted the specific tax dispute. It established, less than a decade after the Constitution's ratification, that the federal government had both the legal authority and the practical willingness to enforce its laws by military force if state and local mechanisms failed or refused to act.[23] Opinion Whether you view that precedent as a necessary foundation for a functioning federal government or as the first real example of federal overreach against ordinary citizens, and I think a fair reading holds room for both reactions, it was Washington personally who set it, at the head of the largest military force he ever commanded as president.

Part Nine

Foreign Policy

By 1793, Washington faced a foreign policy crisis that threatened to drag the young United States into a war it had no ability to survive. Revolutionary France, the country's most important wartime ally, had gone to war with Britain, and under the terms of the 1778 Treaty of Alliance, signed during the Revolutionary War, the United States arguably owed France military support.[24] Public opinion was genuinely split and often intense on the question. Jefferson and much of the country's emerging Democratic-Republican faction saw revolutionary France as a fellow republic fighting the same fight against monarchy that America had just won, and felt a real debt of gratitude for French support during the Revolution. Hamilton and the Federalists viewed the increasingly violent French Revolution with alarm and saw Britain, despite recent hostilities, as America's most important trading partner and a country the young nation could not afford to antagonize militarily.[25]

Washington's assessment was blunt and practical: the United States had no navy to speak of, a tiny standing army, and a treasury just beginning to stabilize under Hamilton's program. Opinion A war with either European power, let alone both, could plausibly have destroyed the country in its first decade. In April 1793, he issued the Neutrality Proclamation, formally declaring that the United States would remain neutral in the conflict between France and Britain and would prosecute any American citizen who provided material aid to either side.[26]

The Constitution doesn't explicitly grant the president unilateral authority to declare neutrality in a foreign war, and Madison, among others, argued at the time that this was a power reserved to Congress. Washington issued it anyway, on his own authority as president. Opinion This is one of the clearest examples in his presidency of him expanding the practical authority of the executive branch through action rather than through any explicit constitutional grant, and it set a precedent for independent presidential authority over foreign affairs that has persisted, and in some ways expanded significantly, ever since.

The neutrality policy was tested almost immediately by the actions of Edmond-Charles Genet, the French Republic's minister to the United States, who arrived in 1793 and began commissioning American privateers to attack British shipping and attempted to raise support for French military expeditions on American soil, all in direct defiance of the neutrality Washington had just declared.[27] When Washington's administration formally requested Genet's recall, Genet reportedly threatened to appeal directly to the American public over the president's head. The episode badly embarrassed Jefferson, who had initially sympathized with France, and contributed to his resignation as Secretary of State at the end of 1793.[28]

The more consequential and controversial foreign policy decision of Washington's presidency came in 1794, when he sent Chief Justice John Jay to negotiate a treaty with Britain to resolve a set of ongoing disputes, including British forts still occupied on American soil in the Northwest Territory in violation of the 1783 Treaty of Paris, and British seizure of American ships trading with France.[29] The resulting Jay Treaty, signed in November 1794, resolved the British forts issue and established a framework for trade between the two countries, but it did not address the British practice of impressment, seizing American sailors and forcing them into British naval service, an omission that infuriated much of the public once the treaty's terms became known.[30]

Opinion The domestic reaction to the Jay Treaty was, by a wide margin, the most hostile public response Washington faced during his entire presidency, and I think it's important to state clearly, because it directly undercuts the idea that Washington was a broadly beloved figure who floated above criticism. Crowds burned Jay in effigy in multiple cities. Hamilton was reportedly struck with a rock while trying to defend the treaty at a public rally in New York.[31] Washington's own image was hung and burned in some protests, an almost unthinkable act against the man who had led the Revolution less than fifteen years earlier.

Despite the backlash, Washington backed the treaty and it was narrowly ratified by the Senate in 1795, and I think the substance of the decision has aged reasonably well: it avoided a war with Britain the United States was in no position to fight, and it kept the country's fragile peace intact during a period when the alternative was a genuinely plausible national catastrophe. Opinion But the political cost was real and immediate, and it's a useful corrective to any narrative that treats Washington's presidency as broadly free of the kind of bitter public hostility that later presidents are more commonly remembered for facing.

Part Ten

Native American Policy

This section covers one of the more difficult parts of Washington's presidency to write about honestly, and I want to be direct about the approach before I get into it. I'm not going to soften what happened, and I'm also not going to strip out the context of what Washington's stated policy actually was, because both matter to an accurate account.

Washington's formal position on Native nations, stated repeatedly in writing throughout his presidency, was that they should be treated as sovereign nations, dealt with through formal treaties rather than simple conquest, and that white settlers who violated those treaties by encroaching on Native land should be punished by federal law.[32] In an April 1791 letter, Washington wrote that "the murder of Indians and their forcible expulsion from their lands" would be dealt with as violations of federal treaties.[33] Opinion On paper, that's a genuinely more restrained and more legally grounded position than a lot of people assume the first American president held, and I think it's worth stating clearly rather than assuming the worst by default.

What happened in practice fell well short of that stated standard, and the gap between the two is the real story of this section. The Northwest Indian War, fought largely during Washington's first term against a confederation of Native nations in the Ohio Territory, including the Miami, Shawnee, and allied tribes, began badly for the United States. In November 1791, a Native confederation led by Miami chief Little Turtle inflicted the single worst defeat the U.S. Army has ever suffered relative to force size, destroying an American force under General Arthur St. Clair near the present-day Ohio-Indiana border. Roughly 630 American soldiers were killed in a single engagement, out of a force of around 1,000.[34] Opinion That's a detail that rarely makes it into popular retellings of the era, and I think it should, because it complicates the picture of this conflict as one-sided from the start. It wasn't.

Washington rebuilt the army under General Anthony Wayne, and in August 1794, American forces decisively defeated the Native confederation at the Battle of Fallen Timbers.[35] The resulting Treaty of Greenville, signed in 1795, forced the Native confederation to cede most of what is now Ohio and part of Indiana to the United States in exchange for treaty payments and formal recognition of their remaining territory.[36] Opinion Like many treaties of this era on both sides of later American history, the Greenville terms did not hold for long. Settlers continued pushing into recognized Native territory in the years that followed, and federal enforcement against those settlers, the same enforcement Washington had promised in writing, was inconsistent at best. The stated policy of protecting treaty boundaries did not survive contact with the actual pace of American westward expansion, under Washington's presidency or the ones that followed it.

Opinion I don't think this record fits neatly into either a story of deliberate, coordinated conquest or a story of good intentions undone entirely by circumstances outside Washington's control. Washington's own writing shows a man who genuinely believed formal treaties and legal process were the correct approach, and who also presided over a government that repeatedly failed to enforce those treaties against its own citizens, resulting in displacement and broken promises to Native nations regardless of what the paperwork said. Both of those things are documented, and I think an honest account has to hold them together rather than picking the one that's easier to write.

Part Eleven

Slavery at Mount Vernon

This is the section I expect readers will scrutinize most closely, and it deserves the same standard I've applied to everything else in this piece: real, descriptive language for what happened, without either minimizing it or stripping out context that matters to an accurate picture.

George Washington inherited his first enslaved people at age eleven, following his father's death, and expanded that number significantly through his 1759 marriage to Martha Custis, whose "dower" enslaved people, technically owned by her late first husband's estate, came under his management at Mount Vernon.[37] By the time of his death in 1799, roughly 317 enslaved people lived and worked at Mount Vernon, of whom Washington personally owned about 123, with the remainder being dower slaves he controlled but could not legally free on his own.[38] They worked the farms, ran the distillery, staffed the household, and made up the labor force that Mount Vernon's agricultural and commercial operations depended on entirely. Opinion I want to state plainly what that means. Washington built and maintained his personal wealth, for the great majority of his adult life, on forced human labor. That's not a matter of interpretation or modern judgment applied retroactively. It's a documented fact of how Mount Vernon functioned as an economic operation, and I'm not going to write around it.

The historical record also shows real, documented discomfort in Washington's own private writing about slavery, particularly later in his life, discomfort that existed alongside his continued practice of owning people. In private letters, he expressed a wish to see slavery gradually abolished by legislative action, writing in 1786 that it was "among my first wishes to see some plan adopted by which slavery in this country may be abolished by slow, sure, and imperceptible degrees."[39] He never introduced or publicly supported such legislation as president. Opinion I think that gap, between private discomfort and public inaction, is itself worth sitting with rather than resolving in his favor or against him. He held real political capital as the country's first president and chose not to spend any of it on the issue publicly, even as he wrote privately that he found the institution troubling.

There's also a documented, uglier side of Washington's direct conduct as a slaveholder that shouldn't be left out. In 1796, while serving as president in Philadelphia, Washington learned that Ona Judge, an enslaved woman who worked as Martha's personal attendant, had escaped to New Hampshire seeking freedom. Washington used his position, and violated the spirit if not the strict letter of a Pennsylvania law that would have granted freedom to enslaved people held in the state for six months, by periodically rotating enslaved members of his household in and out of the state to prevent that law from applying to them.[40] He then pursued Judge's return for years afterward, using intermediaries and government resources to try to recapture her, even after she had started a family in New Hampshire. She was never returned, and evaded Washington's attempts to reclaim her until his death.[41] Opinion This episode, I think, is one of the clearest points where the private discomfort Washington expressed in letters and his actual conduct as a slaveholder diverge sharply. He used the power and resources of the presidency itself in a sustained, personal effort to recapture a woman who had escaped to freedom.

What complicates a purely negative reading, without erasing any of the above, is what Washington did at the end of his life. His will, executed after his death in December 1799, ordered the freedom of all the enslaved people he personally owned, to take effect upon Martha's death, making him the only slaveholding Founding Father among the first five presidents to free the people he enslaved.[42] Martha, reportedly uneasy living surrounded by people who now had a legal incentive to want her dead, freed them within a year of Washington's death instead of waiting.[43] His will also provided for the ongoing support and, where needed, education of the freed people who were young, elderly, or infirm, and explicitly forbade the sale or transport of any of them out of Virginia, a provision aimed at keeping families from being separated.[44] He held no such legal authority over the dower slaves belonging to Martha's estate, who remained enslaved and were eventually redistributed among her Custis heirs after her death.[45]

Opinion I don't think Washington's will amounts to redemption for the decades he spent as a slaveholder, and I'm not going to frame it that way. But I also don't think it should be omitted or treated as a minor footnote, because it was a real, legally binding, and at the time genuinely unusual act among his peers, most of whom, including Jefferson, freed few or none of the people they enslaved. Both facts belong in the same account: he spent most of his life as a slaveholder who personally pursued at least one escaped woman for years, and he ended his life as the only one of the first five presidents to free the people he owned. Neither fact cancels the other out, and I don't think an honest telling should try to make it do so.

Part Twelve

The Birth of Political Parties, Despite Himself

Washington entered his presidency with a genuine belief that organized political factions were dangerous to the young republic, and he tried, deliberately and repeatedly, to keep his administration above that kind of division. He failed, and the failure happened inside his own cabinet, between two of the most talented men he'd appointed.

Thomas Jefferson, his Secretary of State, and Alexander Hamilton, his Secretary of the Treasury, disagreed on nearly everything of consequence: the scope of federal power, the interpretation of the Constitution, the proper relationship with Britain and France, and the direction of the national economy. Opinion I don't think it's an exaggeration to say the modern American two-party system traces its earliest roots directly to this rivalry, playing out inside Washington's own administration while he was actively trying to prevent exactly that outcome.

By 1791 and 1792, the disagreement had moved well beyond private cabinet debate. Jefferson and Madison began organizing what became the Democratic-Republican Party, building support around opposition to Hamilton's financial program and a general suspicion of centralized federal power. Hamilton and his allies coalesced into the Federalist Party, favoring a strong national government, closer ties with Britain, and the financial system he'd built.[46] Jefferson even helped fund a newspaper, Philip Freneau's National Gazette, that regularly published attacks on Hamilton's policies, and by extension on the administration Jefferson himself formally served in.[47] Opinion That's a detail worth sitting with. A sitting cabinet secretary was privately funding a publication that criticized his own president's administration, while continuing to serve in that administration. It's hard to imagine a clearer sign that Washington had lost control of the factional split he was trying to prevent.

Washington's response to the growing rift was to try to broker peace between Jefferson and Hamilton directly, writing to both men in 1792 urging mutual restraint and warning that their public feuding threatened to tear the government apart before it had a real chance to prove itself.[48] Both men wrote back defending themselves and, in substance, blaming the other. Opinion I think this exchange captures something important about the limits of Washington's authority during this period. He had enormous personal credibility, but even he couldn't stop two of the most powerful men in his own government from turning their policy disagreements into the foundation of a permanent partisan divide.

Jefferson resigned as Secretary of State at the end of 1793, worn down by the Genet affair and his losing battles with Hamilton over foreign and domestic policy, and returned to Virginia to continue building the Democratic-Republican opposition from outside the administration. Hamilton resigned from Treasury in early 1795 but remained deeply influential within Federalist circles. By the time Washington left office in 1797, the two-party system he'd spent eight years trying to prevent was fully formed and would decide the very election that succeeded him.[49]

Opinion Washington's Farewell Address, which I'll cover in more detail in the next section, contains his most direct and personal warning about the dangers of political parties, and I think it reads very differently once you understand it wasn't abstract political theory. He'd watched the exact thing he feared happen inside his own administration, between men he'd personally selected and trusted, and he left office without ever finding a way to stop it. Opinion I'd call that one of the clearest failures of his presidency, though I don't think it's fully fair to call it his failure alone. The divide Jefferson and Hamilton represented, over the size and scope of federal power, was arguably inevitable in a country as large and regionally different as the United States. Washington may simply have been the last figure with enough personal standing to hold it together by force of reputation alone, and even he couldn't do it for the full eight years.

Part Thirteen

How Brutal His Press Coverage Actually Was

There's a version of George Washington in American popular memory that treats him as a figure who floated above criticism during his lifetime, universally beloved and untouchable. Opinion I think that version is flatly wrong, and the newspaper record of his second term proves it. By the mid-1790s, Washington was being attacked in print in terms that would sound familiar to anyone following modern American politics, and some of it was genuinely vicious by any era's standards.

The most aggressive opposition came from the Philadelphia Aurora, edited by Benjamin Franklin Bache, Benjamin Franklin's own grandson. Bache's paper accused Washington, by name, of incompetence, financial corruption, and monarchical ambition, and did so repeatedly over years, not as an isolated incident.[50] When Washington announced he would not seek a third term in 1796, the Aurora published an editorial marking his departure that called him the source of the country's ills and expressed relief that "a man who was the source of all the misfortunes of our country" was finally leaving office, language that reads as startlingly hostile for a figure now remembered as nearly beyond reproach.[51]

Opinion I think it's worth being specific about the range of accusations, because "he faced criticism" understates how personal and severe it actually got. Bache and other opposition writers accused Washington of deliberately mismanaging military and financial affairs, compared him unfavorably to a common criminal in print, and, perhaps most strikingly, suggested he was suffering from declining mental faculties or had never been particularly capable in the first place, undercutting the very reputation for competence that had gotten him elected unanimously twice.[52] Some pieces went further and accused him of harboring secret monarchical ambitions, a charge that, given everything covered in this piece about his voluntary relinquishing of power, doesn't hold up well to the historical record, but was nonetheless printed and circulated as a serious claim at the time.

The Jay Treaty controversy, covered in Part Nine, produced some of the sharpest attacks. Washington was burned in effigy in multiple cities, an act of public political theater normally reserved for deeply unpopular figures, not a revered founding president.[53] Thomas Paine, whose pamphlet Common Sense had helped ignite the Revolution and who Washington had once tried to help free from a French prison, published a scathing open letter to Washington in 1796 accusing him of abandoning republican principles and, in one particularly harsh passage, questioning whether Washington had any real talents at all beyond good luck.[54] Opinion That detail matters to me specifically because Paine wasn't some marginal crank. He was one of the most important voices of the Revolution itself, and by the mid-1790s he was writing about Washington in language closer to contempt than disagreement.

Washington, by most accounts from those close to him, took this criticism personally and it wore on him. At a heated cabinet meeting in August 1793, prompted by a mock pasquinade that depicted him on a guillotine, Jefferson's private notes record Washington declaring that he would rather be in his grave than in his present situation, and that he would rather be on his farm than be made emperor of the world, even as he was being accused of wanting to be a king.[55] Three years later, writing directly to Jefferson in July 1796 after their political relationship had broken down, Washington complained in his own hand that every act of his administration had been misrepresented in the press "in such exagerated, and indecent terms as could scarcely be applied to a Nero, a notorious defaulter, or even a common pickpocket."[55] Opinion I think those two moments, three years apart, one a furious outburst in front of his own cabinet and the other a bitter line in a private letter to a man he'd once trusted completely, should put to rest any idea that Washington's presidency was some kind of universally celebrated victory lap. He left office in 1797 having experienced, by his own account on more than one occasion, press treatment he found personally degrading, not a minor cost of public life he simply shrugged off.

Opinion I want to draw a direct line here to the broader premise of this series. The idea that today's political climate is uniquely toxic, and that earlier eras of American politics were somehow more civil or more respectful of their leaders, doesn't survive contact with how the country's most revered founding figure was actually written about while he held office. He was called corrupt, incompetent, senile, and secretly monarchical, by serious and prominent people, in widely read publications, while serving as president. That doesn't make current political discourse healthy by comparison. It does mean the nostalgia for a more civil past is, at least in Washington's case, not well supported by the actual historical record.

Part Fourteen

The Farewell Address

By 1796, Washington had made clear in private for some time that he intended to step down after two terms, and the document he used to formally announce that decision to the public became one of the most studied pieces of political writing in American history.

The Farewell Address was published as a letter in newspapers on September 19, 1796, rather than delivered as a speech.[56] It was primarily drafted by Alexander Hamilton, working from an earlier draft Washington himself had prepared with James Madison's help back in 1792, when Washington had briefly considered not seeking a second term at all.[57] Opinion I think that origin is worth noting, because it shows the Address wasn't a hurried farewell statement. Washington had been thinking about what he wanted to say to the country upon leaving office for close to four years before he actually said it.

The Address is remembered today mostly for two specific warnings, and both of them read as strikingly prescient given everything covered in the previous three sections of this piece. The first was his warning against the dangers of political parties, what he called "the baneful effects of the spirit of party," arguing that factionalism would distract the government from the public good, foment jealousy between regions, and potentially open the door to foreign influence over domestic politics.[58] Opinion Having just covered, in Part Twelve, how thoroughly that exact process played out inside his own cabinet between Jefferson and Hamilton, this warning doesn't read like abstract political philosophy. It reads like a firsthand account of something Washington had watched happen in real time, to people he had personally chosen and trusted, and had been unable to stop.

The second major warning concerned foreign policy, and Washington's language here is frequently misquoted or oversimplified in modern political debate. He did not argue for total isolation from the world. He argued specifically against permanent alliances with any single foreign power, writing that the nation should extend commercial relations with all countries while having "as little political connection as possible" with any of them, and warning that permanent alliances tend to drag a country into conflicts that serve another nation's interests rather than its own.[59] Opinion Given the foreign policy chapter covered in Part Nine, particularly the near-war crisis with France and the domestic firestorm over the Jay Treaty with Britain, this warning reads as a direct lesson Washington drew from his own two terms of trying to keep the young country out of European conflicts it had no capacity to survive.

The Address also included warnings that get less attention but are worth including here. Washington cautioned against excessive public debt, urged the country to cultivate religion and morality as necessary foundations for public virtue and stable government, and warned specifically against regional and sectional loyalty superseding loyalty to the union as a whole, a warning that would prove tragically accurate less than seventy years later.[60]

Opinion I think the most honest way to evaluate the Farewell Address is to ask whether the country actually listened, and the answer is mixed at best. The warning against political parties was essentially ignored within Washington's own lifetime, as covered in Part Twelve, and the two-party system has been a permanent fixture of American politics ever since, for better or worse. The warning against permanent foreign alliances held for well over a century, arguably until the World Wars and the Cold War fundamentally reshaped America's role in the world, at which point the country abandoned that principle more or less completely. Opinion I don't think that makes the Address a failure. I think it means Washington correctly identified the two forces, faction and foreign entanglement, that he believed posed the greatest long-term threats to the republic he'd helped build, even though he couldn't stop either one from eventually taking hold. Whether the country was right to eventually abandon both warnings is a separate question this piece isn't set up to answer, but the fact that he saw both coming, this clearly, this early, is itself remarkable.

Part Fifteen

Death and Immediate Aftermath

Washington's retirement after leaving the presidency in March 1797 lasted less than three years. He returned to Mount Vernon intending to focus on farming and rebuilding an estate he felt had suffered from his long absences in public service, and largely stayed out of active politics, though he briefly agreed to accept command of a provisional army in 1798 during a period of tension with France known as the Quasi-War, a command that never saw active deployment before the crisis cooled.[61]

On December 12, 1799, Washington spent several hours outdoors on horseback inspecting his farms in cold, wet weather, including snow and freezing rain. He returned home that evening without changing out of his wet clothes. By the next day he had developed a severe sore throat, and his condition worsened rapidly overnight.[62] Modern medical historians reviewing the case have generally concluded he was likely suffering from acute epiglottitis or a similar severe upper airway infection, a condition that is readily treatable today but was essentially untreatable, and poorly understood, by the medicine of 1799.[63]

The treatment Washington received from his physicians almost certainly hastened his death rather than helping him. Following standard medical practice of the era, doctors bled him repeatedly over the course of his illness, withdrawing an estimated five pints of blood total, roughly half of his body's total blood volume, in addition to other aggressive treatments intended to induce vomiting and blistering.[64] Opinion By modern medical understanding, this treatment almost certainly weakened him at the exact moment his body needed strength to fight a severe infection. Washington himself reportedly told his physicians at one point to stop, saying he felt he was dying and did not wish to be given more medicine that wasn't helping, though the bleeding continued regardless.[65]

Washington died at Mount Vernon on the night of December 14, 1799, at around 10 p.m., surrounded by his wife Martha and close household members. His last recorded words, spoken to his secretary Tobias Lear, were reportedly "'Tis well," after confirming that instructions for his burial had been properly understood.[66]

News of his death traveled slowly by the standards of any modern comparison, but the public reaction, once it did spread, was immense and immediate. Congress, in session in Philadelphia, adjourned for the day upon receiving the news. Henry Lee, a Revolutionary War cavalry officer and father of future Confederate general Robert E. Lee, delivered a eulogy before Congress that produced the phrase most associated with Washington's memory ever since, describing him as "first in war, first in peace, and first in the hearts of his countrymen."[67] Opinion That phrase, more than almost anything else, is responsible for cementing the largely uncomplicated, heroic version of Washington that dominated American memory for the following two centuries, the version this piece has spent considerable effort complicating with the messier details of his actual presidency.

The mythologizing began almost immediately and accelerated quickly. Mason Locke Weems, an itinerant book peddler and minister, published a wildly popular biography of Washington in 1800, expanded in later editions, that included largely fabricated stories intended to illustrate Washington's virtue, most famously the cherry tree anecdote in which a young Washington confesses to chopping down his father's tree because he cannot tell a lie.[68] There is no credible historical evidence this event occurred, and Weems himself never claimed a specific source for it beyond vague reference to an elderly family friend. Opinion I think it's worth stating plainly that the single most famous story most Americans know about George Washington's childhood is almost certainly fiction, invented within a year of his death by a book seller trying to sell copies, and that fictional version proved far more durable in popular memory than the extensively documented, considerably more complicated real man this piece has tried to describe.

Washington was buried at Mount Vernon according to his own wishes, and his will, as covered in Part Eleven, took effect with his death, ordering the eventual freedom of the enslaved people he personally owned. Opinion It's a fitting, if imperfect, final note on a presidency defined throughout this piece by exactly that pattern: genuine, historically significant accomplishment sitting directly alongside serious, well-documented failure, refusing to resolve neatly into either a hero's story or a villain's story.

Part Sixteen: The Modern Verdict

The Modern Verdict

Every piece in this series is going to end here, with the same three questions applied to every president: would this person be a polarizing or unifying figure if they were on the political stage today, what about their record holds up under modern scrutiny, and what wouldn't survive contact with today's press and social media the way it survived the press of their own era.

Before I answer these three questions, I want to be precise about what this section is actually asking, because it matters. This is not a hypothetical where George Washington, slaveholder, runs for president in 2026 exactly as he was in 1789. If that were the question, the answer isn't complicated: a candidate who currently owned other human beings would be instantly disqualified in modern American politics, correctly and by a landslide, and no amount of military record or restraint in office would overcome that. Opinion I'm not interested in pretending otherwise, and I'm not going to write around it to make the rest of this section easier.

What this section is actually asking is narrower and, I think, more useful: if you take Washington's political character, his stated positions, his instincts on federal power, faction, debt, and foreign entanglement, and ask how that specific combination would be received by the modern electorate, separate from the fact that he lived in and participated in an institution the country would later abolish, what do you get. That's a question about political philosophy, not a question about whether the historical man himself, with his full biography, could win an election today. He couldn't, and I don't think the piece should imply otherwise.

Would he be polarizing or unifying today?

Judged on his politics specifically, restraint in the use of power, suspicion of factionalism, caution about federal debt, resistance to permanent foreign entanglement, I think Washington lands closer to unifying than almost anyone else this series will cover. Opinion He'd draw strong support from the political right on the strength of his military record and his instincts against overreach, and I think he'd also pull real support from moderates and parts of the center-left, because his actual governing record, an activist federal financial program, a national bank, federal troops deployed to enforce tax law, doesn't map cleanly onto either modern party's current platform. Opinion He doesn't fit neatly into either coalition, and that's exactly why I think his politics, evaluated on their own, would be less polarizing than almost anyone in modern American politics.

What holds up under modern scrutiny?

His resignation of military command, twice, remains one of the most significant acts of voluntary restraint by anyone who has ever held that much power in human history, and that holds up completely. His insistence on civilian control of the military holds up. His approach to the national bank decision, soliciting serious written arguments from both Jefferson and Hamilton before ruling, holds up as a model of deliberate governance.

Opinion And his warnings in the Farewell Address hold up about as well as anything in American political history. He warned that unchecked factionalism would consume the government's ability to function, and current American politics is a difficult case to argue against that warning. He warned against permanent foreign entanglement, and decades of American foreign policy since have largely proven that concern justified. He warned against excessive public debt, and the national debt today is a serious, documented problem by any measure. He was describing a country that was too big, too indebted, too divided, and too entangled abroad, at a moment when the United States was barely a decade old and none of those problems existed yet in serious form. That he named all four this early, and that all four remain legitimate problems today, is a genuinely remarkable track record for a two-hundred-year-old warning.

What wouldn't survive contact with today's press and social media?

Opinion The fact of having owned enslaved people at all would be, on its own, immediately disqualifying for anyone running for office today, and I want that stated as plainly as everything else in this section rather than left as a soft aside. Beyond that baseline, if you're asking which specific documented conduct would generate the most damaging coverage even within his own historical context, it's the Ona Judge pursuit. A president personally directing years of government resources toward recapturing a specific woman who had escaped slavery, using his own letters as the evidence, is the kind of story that would dominate news cycles for weeks, and that scrutiny would be entirely fair. The broken promises tied to the Treaty of Greenville and the pattern of failed federal enforcement of Native treaty boundaries would draw serious, sustained criticism as well, especially set against his own written commitments to enforce them.

Opinion So the honest verdict is a layered one, not a single clean answer. As a historical figure judged in full, slavery is a permanent, disqualifying stain that no other part of his record offsets, and I'm not going to write a version of this piece that suggests otherwise. But as a political philosophy, separated from that specific historical fact and evaluated on its own terms, restraint, suspicion of his own power, wariness of faction, debt, and foreign entanglement, I think it's a combination the country could genuinely use right now, and I think his specific warnings about those four things turning out to be accurate, more than two centuries later, is worth taking seriously on its own merits.
