Turkey Is the Next Iran. So Is Your NATO Membership Worth More to Washington Than You Think?

A NATO member's president called Netanyahu the Hitler of our time. Netanyahu called Erdogan a Kurdish killer sheltering Iran's proxies. A former US counterterrorism chief says the real reason Washington might leave NATO has nothing to do with saving money.

Part One

The Strike That Wasn't a Surprise

I want to start with something that happened only a couple of days before I sat down to write this, because it tells you everything about the timing of what I am about to lay out.

On August 19, 2026, Israeli warplanes bombed a base in northern Syria, just a short drive from the Turkish border. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said Turkey had been trying to establish a military presence there and that after Israel's warnings were ignored, Israel had made sure the message was understood.[1] In his own post, Netanyahu put it in two parts: “We made the message clear: Don't,” he wrote, adding that “it seems they didn't hear it well enough, so we made sure they'd understand it better.”[2] That is a translated rendering of his own words, not my characterization of them. That is the Israeli Prime Minister, on the record, describing an airstrike near a NATO member's border as a message.

In his own words

“We made the message clear: Don't. It seems they didn't hear it well enough, so we made sure they'd understand it better.” That is Netanyahu describing an airstrike near the Turkish border, days before this piece was written.

I am writing this now because Netanyahu is facing a tough re election fight, and after more than two years of ordering strikes on Israel's enemies across the Middle East, he appears to be zeroing in on a new one: Turkey.[3] A Likud campaign billboard in Tel Aviv reportedly depicted Iran's supreme leader, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan, and Hezbollah's Secretary General side by side, with Hebrew text reading “They want Netanyahu to lose.”[4]

Let that sink in. An elected New York City mayor, alongside the leader of a NATO member state, positioned on the same billboard as the heads of Iran and Hezbollah. That is not policy analysis. That is a campaign strategy, and it is one I think Americans need to understand before it drags us somewhere none of us voted for.

Because here is the thing that should stop you cold: Joe Kent, who served as Director of the National Counterterrorism Center under the Trump administration, has stated that Trump's desire to withdraw from NATO is not solely motivated by financial concerns. Rather, it stems from a strategic intent to dissolve the constraints of Article 5, thereby enabling the United States to intervene in support of Israel in the event of a military confrontation with Turkey.[5]

I am not a foreign policy analyst. I am a 25 year old who grew up watching NATO get talked about as this untouchable pillar of American security, something that stopped a third world war and kept it stopped for eighty years. And now I am watching people who used to run parts of our national security apparatus openly floating the idea that we might blow the whole thing up, not to protect Americans, but to clear the way for one country to fight another country that is currently our ally.

This piece is about how we got to that point, who is saying what, who is pushing back, and what it would actually mean if the rhetoric turns into something real. I am not interested in picking a side between Israel and Turkey. I am interested in what happens to the rest of us if two governments drag an alliance built to protect Americans into becoming a tool for a fight that was never ours to begin with.

Part Two

How We Got Here

If you only started paying attention to Turkey and Israel this week, I understand why. But this fracture did not happen overnight, and I think it is important you see the timeline so you understand this is not some sudden invention of election season. It has been building for over a decade, and it has accelerated hard in the last three years.

Go back to May 2010. Israeli marines stormed the Mavi Marmara, a Turkish aid ship trying to break the naval blockade on Gaza, and nine Turkish citizens were killed in the clashes that followed.[6] That single event cracked what had been, up to that point, a genuinely close relationship. Turkey was the first Muslim majority country to ever recognize Israel, and the two countries had shared intelligence and conducted joint military exercises for years.[7] After Mavi Marmara, Turkey expelled Israel's ambassador and froze military cooperation.

The relationship limped along for over a decade with periods of partial thaw. In September 2023, Erdogan and Netanyahu even met and shook hands in New York, the first time the two leaders had done so. That attempt at restoration collapsed within a month, when the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack on Israel triggered the war in Gaza.[8] Erdogan became one of Israel's most vocal critics on the world stage, and by November 2023 he was telling Turkish media “Netanyahu is no longer someone we can talk to. We have written him off.”[9]

From there, the deterioration became structural rather than just rhetorical. In May 2024, Turkey suspended all direct trade with Israel over its conduct in Gaza.[10] That was not a symbolic gesture. Turkey further tightened those restrictions in February 2026 by halting the issuance of Eur-Med certificates, the customs documents that had let Turkish goods reach Israel duty free through Europe.[11] This is a NATO member actively severing its economic relationship with a close American security partner in the region.

The rhetoric kept escalating alongside the economic break. By April 2026, in the middle of the US and Israeli war on Iran, Netanyahu accused Erdogan on X of “massacring his own Kurdish citizens” and “accommodating Iran's terror regime and its proxies.”[12] Turkey's Foreign Ministry responded in kind, stating that Netanyahu “has been described as the Hitler of our time due to the crimes he has committed,” and accusing Israel of manufacturing Turkey as its next enemy.[13]

Then came the legal escalation. Istanbul's chief prosecutor filed indictments against Netanyahu and 34 other Israeli officials, including Defense Minister Israel Katz, Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, and IDF Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir, over Israel's naval interception of the Global Sumud Flotilla attempting to deliver humanitarian aid to Gaza in late 2025.[14] By July 2026, that indictment had produced formal arrest warrants from an Istanbul court, and within weeks Turkey's Justice Ministry had formally requested INTERPOL Red Notices for Netanyahu, tied to the flotilla interception and the detention of hundreds of activists aboard those ships.[15]

I want to pause here and be direct with you the way I try to be in every piece I write. I am not going to pretend Turkey's hands are entirely clean in this relationship either. Erdogan has his own domestic reasons to posture against Israel, and Turkish rhetoric calling Netanyahu “Hitler” is not measured diplomatic language, it is inflammatory in exactly the same way Israeli rhetoric about Turkey has become. This is not a story where one government is behaving reasonably and the other is not. It is two governments, each with their own domestic political incentives, escalating against each other in public, in real time, while both happen to be entangled with the United States in ways that could pull us into something we did not choose.

That is the environment the “Turkey is the next Iran” rhetoric emerged from. It did not come out of nowhere. It came out of sixteen years of a relationship falling apart in stages, with the last three years accelerating that collapse dramatically. In Part Three, I am going to walk you through exactly how Israeli officials started framing Turkey as the next major target, in their own words.

Part Three

The “Next Iran” Doctrine, In Their Own Words

I do not want you to take my word for any of this. I want you to hear it from the people actually saying it, because once you see how explicit this rhetoric has become, you will understand why I think it deserves more attention than it has gotten.

The clearest starting point is February 17, 2026. Former Israeli Prime Minister Naftali Bennett spoke at the Conference of Presidents of Major American Jewish Organizations and did not hedge his language at all. He declared bluntly that “a new Turkish threat is emerging” and that “Turkey is the new Iran.”[16] He went further than that single line too, warning that Erdogan is “sophisticated, dangerous,” and accusing Ankara of trying to “flip Saudi Arabia” against Israel while building a “hostile Sunni axis with nuclear Pakistan.”[17]

I want you to sit with that phrase for a second: hostile Sunni axis with nuclear Pakistan. That is not a minor diplomatic complaint about trade restrictions or flotillas. That is language designed to put Turkey in the same threat category as Iran, a country the United States and Israel were actively at war with when Bennett said it.

Netanyahu did not stay quiet after Bennett's remarks either. He responded to the same set of anxieties by outlining what reporting has described as a “hexagon of alliances,” including India, Greece, Cyprus, and unspecified partners across the Arab world, Africa, and Asia, explicitly designed to constrain Turkey's regional influence.[18] In the months surrounding that announcement, Israel hosted the leaders of Greece, Cyprus, and India specifically to cement that alliance structure. Standing next to the Greek and Cypriot leaders in December 2025, Netanyahu delivered a line that was widely interpreted as directed at Turkey, warning that “those who fantasise they can re-establish their empires and their dominion over our lands” should abandon that ambition.[19]

That is not subtle. Greece and Cyprus both have their own long running territorial and historical disputes with Turkey. Building an alliance explicitly around those two countries, framed against a backdrop of empire and dominion, is about as clear a signal as diplomatic language gets without using Turkey's name in the sentence.

By April 2026, the diplomatic hedging disappeared entirely. Netanyahu posted directly on X, naming Erdogan and accusing him of “massacring his own Kurdish citizens” and “accommodating Iran's terror regime and its proxies,” while undermining regional stability.[20] This is a sitting head of government publicly labeling a NATO member's president in terms usually reserved for adversarial states.

Here is where I think it is important to be fair, because that is the entire premise of this publication. Not everyone inside Israeli political and foreign policy circles agrees with this framing, and some of the pushback is coming from people with real standing to say so. I am going to walk you through exactly who is contesting this “next Iran” doctrine, and why, in Part Four. Because if Israeli officials themselves are questioning whether this rhetoric is genuine strategy or election season convenience, American readers deserve to hear that side of it too before anyone starts talking about NATO membership as collateral damage.

Part Four

The Skeptics Inside the Tent

Here is the part of this story that I think matters most for keeping this piece honest, and it is the part that gets left out of a lot of coverage I have seen on this topic. The “Turkey is the next Iran” framing is not universally accepted, even among Israeli officials, American diplomats, and foreign policy analysts who are broadly sympathetic to Israel. Some of the sharpest pushback is coming from inside the tent.

Start with Alon Pinkas, a former Israeli ambassador who has no obvious incentive to undercut his own government's messaging. He put it plainly: politicians like Naftali Bennett and Benjamin Netanyahu rely on the perpetual threat of war.[21] That is a former Israeli diplomat naming, out loud, the possibility that this entire framing serves a political function rather than reflecting a genuine strategic assessment.

Yossi Mekelberg of Chatham House made a similar point using different language. He argued Turkey is “so much noise” relative to the actual threat Iran posed, and warned that treating rhetorical friction as a strategic threat risks turning Turkey into a genuine opponent simply through the force of Israel's own threat inflation.[22] That is worth reading twice. A respected Middle East analyst is warning that the “next Iran” rhetoric itself could become a self fulfilling prophecy, manufacturing the very conflict it claims to be warning about.

Reporting on this dynamic has also been blunt about who benefits from the narrative. Bennett benefits because he is running for prime minister and needs a threat to run against. Netanyahu benefits because a new Sunni bogeyman justifies the alliance architecture he has been assembling. And certain Washington think tanks benefit too, since threat inflation has long been a profitable business in Washington.[23] I am not the one making that accusation. That is how it was reported, and I think it deserves to be taken seriously precisely because it lines up with a pattern I have written about before on this site: officials manufacturing urgency around a threat in ways that happen to serve their own political survival.

It is not only Israeli voices raising doubts. The Trump administration's own envoy to the region has pushed back too. US Special Envoy for Syria and Ambassador to Turkey Tom Barrack downplayed the escalating tensions as just “rhetoric,” pushing instead for cooperation between Turkey and Israel on security and energy projects. Barrack went further, stating plainly that “Turkey is just not a country to be messed with.”[24] That is a sitting American ambassador, appointed by the same administration facing pressure to abandon NATO, explicitly rejecting the idea that Turkey should be treated as the next adversary.

I want to be fair to the other side of this too, because dismissing the “next Iran” framing entirely would be its own kind of bias. Some of the underlying concerns are not purely manufactured. Turkey's growing influence in Syria and Gaza is real, and there have been persistent rumors of Ankara attempting to pull Saudi Arabia away from its rapprochement with Israel. Steven Cook of the Council on Foreign Relations has laid out what he considers a legitimate worst case scenario, warning that if Turkey succeeds in swinging Saudi Arabia or solidifying ties with nuclear armed Pakistan, the strategic map changes overnight.[25]

Opinion So here is where I land on this section, and I want to be clear this is my read rather than a settled fact. There is likely a real strategic shift happening in the region, with Turkey's growing influence giving Israeli planners a legitimate reason for concern. But I do not think the “next Iran” framing can be dismissed as pure political theater either. Netanyahu's warnings have a track record of preceding action, not just noise. Iran went from rhetorical target to active war. Hamas leadership in Doha went from a stated threat to an airstrike. The Syrian base near the Turkish border went from a warning to a strike within days. Whatever mix of genuine strategy and political convenience is driving the “next Iran” rhetoric, I do not think Americans should assume this stays at the level of talk. The pattern suggests otherwise, and that is exactly why the NATO question in the sections ahead matters as much as it does.

In Part Five, I am going to walk through how Turkey has responded to all of this, because the escalation has not been one directional.

Part Five

Turkey's Response

If Part Three showed you how Israeli officials have framed Turkey as the next threat, this section shows you that Turkey has not been sitting quietly while that framing builds. The escalation here runs in both directions, and I think it is important you see Ankara's side of the ledger too before we get into what any of this means for NATO.

The most direct diplomatic shot Turkey has fired came through its legal system. Istanbul's chief prosecutor filed indictments against Netanyahu and 34 other Israeli officials, including Defense Minister Israel Katz, Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant, National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir, IDF Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir, and former Mossad chief Yossi Cohen.[26] These charges stemmed from Israel's naval interception of dozens of vessels in the Global Sumud Flotilla, which was attempting to deliver humanitarian aid to Gaza in late September and early October 2025. Israel detained and later deported everyone aboard those 39 boats, including Swedish activist Greta Thunberg and two dozen Turkish citizens.[27]

That indictment escalated further through the middle of 2026. An Istanbul court issued arrest warrants for Netanyahu and a co-defendant on July 14, and by August, Turkey's Justice Minister Akin Gurlek had announced that the Justice Ministry formally requested INTERPOL Red Notices for Netanyahu, specifically tied to the flotilla interception and the detention of the activists aboard.[28] It is worth noting this was not Turkey's first legal action against Netanyahu either. Turkey had already issued warrants against Netanyahu and other top officials over what it characterized as genocide connected to Israel's military campaign in Gaza.[29] This is a NATO member state's own justice system formally pursuing an arrest warrant against the head of government of a close American security partner. I do not think that fact should be glossed over, regardless of where you land on the underlying merits of the case.

Worth sitting with

A NATO member's own court system has issued an arrest warrant, and its justice ministry has formally requested an INTERPOL Red Notice, for the sitting head of government of a country the United States treats as its closest Middle East ally.

The diplomatic language from Ankara has matched the legal escalation. Turkey's Foreign Ministry described Netanyahu as “the Hitler of our time,”[30] language I flagged in Part Two as being every bit as inflammatory as anything coming out of Israeli political circles. Erdogan himself has been consistent on this front going back years, telling Turkish media as early as November 2023 that he had written Netanyahu off entirely over the conduct of the Gaza war.

Turkey has also pushed back specifically against the “next Iran” framing itself, not just against Israel's conduct in Gaza. When Israeli officials began circulating that comparison, a statement from the Turkish president's office condemned what it called an attempt “to present Turkey as a new threat.”[31] In other words, Ankara is not oblivious to the doctrine being built around it. Turkish officials see the pattern too, and they are naming it in public.

I want to be straightforward about something here, the same way I was in Part Two. I am not going to pretend Turkey is a passive victim in this relationship. A government pursuing international arrest warrants against a foreign head of state, while its own officials use Holocaust comparisons in public statements, is not conducting itself with restraint either. Both governments are escalating. Both are using inflammatory language. And both, I would argue, have their own domestic political incentives for doing so, Netanyahu with his re election fight, Erdogan with his own standing as a regional power broker positioning himself against Israel on the world stage.

What I keep coming back to is this. None of that domestic political calculus on either side changes what happens to the rest of us if this escalates past rhetoric and legal warrants into something resembling direct confrontation. That is where the NATO question becomes unavoidable, and that is exactly where I am taking this next.

Part Six

The NATO Wildcard, Joe Kent's Warning

Everything up to this point has been about Israel and Turkey. This section is about why you, an American reading this, should care about any of it beyond simple foreign policy interest. Because the connective tissue between that regional rivalry and your own life runs directly through NATO, and the person who laid that connection out most explicitly is someone who used to run American counterterrorism policy at the highest level.

Joe Kent served as Director of the National Counterterrorism Center under the Trump administration. He served roughly twenty years in the US military before later working within the CIA, and during Trump's 2020 campaign he served as a counterterrorism adviser, developing close ties with Trump personally.[32] This is not some outside critic. This is someone who sat inside the administration currently threatening to pull the United States out of NATO.

Kent resigned from his position in March 2026. He cited opposition to the US Israeli war on Iran, which began in late February, saying Iran posed “no imminent threat” to the United States, and claimed the administration started the war due to pressure from Israel and its powerful American lobby.[33] With that resignation, he became the most high profile figure inside the Trump administration to publicly break with the White House over the Iran war.

Then, responding directly to Trump's own criticism of NATO on Truth Social, Kent posted the line that I think deserves far more attention than it has gotten. “Unfortunately leaving NATO won't be to avoid foreign entanglements, we'll be leaving NATO so we can side with Israel when Turkey and Israel eventually clash in Syria.”[34]

In his own words

“Unfortunately leaving NATO won't be to avoid foreign entanglements, we'll be leaving NATO so we can side with Israel when Turkey and Israel eventually clash in Syria.” That is a former Director of the National Counterterrorism Center, on the record.

Read that again. A former director of the National Counterterrorism Center is not speculating that the US might leave NATO for cost sharing reasons, or because European allies are not pulling their weight, which is the argument Trump has publicly made for years. Kent is saying the actual strategic purpose behind a US exit would be to free Washington from Article 5 obligations specifically so it can back Israel if Israel and Turkey, a fellow NATO member, end up in direct conflict inside Syria.

Kent did not stop there either. He also criticized Washington's broader approach in Syria, arguing the US helped remove the country's former government and in doing so empowered a leader with alleged extremist ties. “Time to stop playing arsonist and fireman in the Middle East, it's just not worth it,” he wrote.[35]

I want to sit with that phrase, arsonist and fireman, because I think it captures something I have been circling throughout this entire piece. A former top American counterterrorism official is describing US Middle East policy as a cycle where American action helps create the fires it then claims credit for putting out.

Trump's own comments give Kent's warning more weight rather than less. Asked directly whether he would reconsider NATO membership, Trump responded that it was “beyond reconsideration,” adding that he was never swayed by NATO and always considered it a “paper tiger.”[36] That is not a hypothetical discussion happening in think tank papers. That is the sitting American president, on the record, describing the alliance in dismissive terms while his own former counterterrorism chief is telling you exactly why he thinks that dismissal is happening now.

Opinion Here is my read on this, and I am labeling it clearly as my own opinion rather than settled fact. I think Kent's framing is the single most important piece of information in this entire investigation, because it reframes NATO membership from something that protects Americans into something that could become an obstacle to a foreign policy goal that has nothing to do with American security. If Kent is right, the exit strategy is not isolationism. It is the opposite of isolationism. It is clearing a path toward deeper regional entanglement, just without the alliance structure that has historically restrained unilateral action.

Part Seven

What Article 5 Actually Says

I said at the end of Part Six that I want to walk through what Article 5 of the NATO treaty actually says, because I think most Americans, myself included before I researched this piece, carry around an assumption about that clause that is not quite accurate.

The common understanding is simple. An attack on one NATO member is treated as an attack on all of them, and the alliance responds militarily as one. That is the version most of us grew up hearing, and it is the version that makes NATO membership feel like an ironclad guarantee of protection. It is also, according to the actual legal analysis, not entirely true.

The treaty text allows each member state to take “such action as it deems necessary,” a phrase that can range anywhere from military measures to purely political gestures.[37] That single clause is the entire gap. It means Article 5 does not obligate any member state to send troops, aircraft, or weapons. It means a country can invoke Article 5, formally acknowledge that an attack occurred, and then respond with nothing more than a diplomatic statement of concern.

Even in the event of a direct attack, members are not obliged to respond with force, and Ankara's own complicated history with NATO shows the alliance's collective defense clause does not automatically prevent a member from being left without meaningful support.[37] Think about what that means in the specific scenario Joe Kent laid out. If Israel and Turkey were to clash and the United States had already exited NATO, that removes America from the equation entirely, no vote, no obligation, no discussion. But even if the US stayed in NATO, Article 5 alone does not guarantee Turkey gets the kind of backing most Americans assume it would.

The reporting on the Qatar strike makes this even more concrete. Analysts have warned that Sweden and Finland, still frustrated over what they characterized as Ankara's “extortion” during their own NATO accession process, could block any collective Article 5 response involving Turkey. The United States, as Israel's closest ally, could also veto an Article 5 trigger if Turkey were portrayed as a sponsor of terrorism.[38] I want you to notice how many off ramps exist here. This is not one clean mechanism that either activates or does not. It is a consensus based system with multiple members who each have their own reasons to withhold support, and Turkey knows this.

That is precisely why Turkish officials have started pushing the issue publicly rather than assuming the shield is automatic. Turkish presidential adviser Gulnur Aybet stated directly that Ankara does not question the viability of Article 5, and in fact expects it to be fulfilled, arguing that “a NATO that is fit for purpose would acknowledge this existential threat to Turkey.”[39] Read between the lines of that statement. A senior Turkish official is publicly asserting that Article 5 should apply to their situation, which only makes sense as a statement if there is real uncertainty about whether it actually would.

There is also polling data that captures this uncertainty from inside Turkey itself. A survey from the Istanbul based IstanPol think tank found that only 40.2 percent of Turkish respondents believed NATO would side with Turkey in a crisis involving Israel, while 34.3 percent disagreed, and another 25.4 percent were neutral or had no opinion.[40] That is a country whose own citizens are roughly split on whether the alliance they belong to would actually defend them against the specific threat this piece has been documenting. Despite that uncertainty, 64.3 percent of the same respondents still said NATO membership benefits Turkey's overall security.

So here is where that leaves us heading into the next section. Article 5 is not the guarantee most Americans assume it is. It is a framework built on consensus, full of legal off ramps, and Turkey's own population is not confident it would hold in exactly the scenario Joe Kent described. Combine that legal reality with a sitting president calling NATO a paper tiger and a former counterterrorism chief predicting a US exit specifically to clear the path for backing Israel, and you start to see why I think this deserves more attention than it has gotten.

Part Eight

What Americans Actually Think

Everything I have walked you through so far, the Israeli officials framing Turkey as the next Iran, Turkey's legal counterattacks, Joe Kent's warning about why the US might really leave NATO, the legal reality that Article 5 does not guarantee what most of us assume it does, all of it raises one obvious question. What do the American people who would actually bear the consequences of this think about staying in NATO in the first place?

The polling on this is remarkably consistent, and it cuts directly against the idea that abandoning the alliance is something Americans broadly want.

Start with the Chicago Council on Global Affairs, which has been tracking American attitudes toward NATO since 1974. Their most recent Ipsos polling found a majority of Americans remain supportive of NATO, broadly consistent with a five decade trend, even as fewer respondents now feel confident that European allies would help defend the United States if it came to that.[41]

Pew Research backs this up with similar numbers. A majority of Americans continue to hold a favorable opinion of NATO, and about two thirds of US adults see a real benefit to membership, even though that favorability has dipped slightly over the past year, driven mostly by declining support among Republicans.[42]

The Annenberg Public Policy Center's survey, conducted between February and March 2026 with a nationally representative sample, found something similar from a different angle. A majority of US adults, 61 percent, said that remaining a member of NATO is at least moderately important, and just over half said US membership provides at least a moderate security benefit.[43]

73% of Americans say remaining in NATO is important to US security, including 64 percent of Republicans and 61 percent of self identified MAGA Republicans, per the Reagan Institute's June 2026 Summer Survey.[44]

Let that number sit for a second. This is not a poll of Democrats or foreign policy elites. This is a conservative leaning institution finding that a majority of the president's own base wants to stay in NATO, even as the president himself is publicly calling it a paper tiger.

Gallup's numbers push this even further. Gallup polling showed more than three quarters of Americans supported either increasing or maintaining the current US commitment to NATO, the highest combined total in Gallup's polling history dating back to 1998. That same polling found about six in ten Republicans supported increasing or maintaining the current commitment, up from less than half in 2022, while only 13 percent of Republicans wanted to withdraw from the alliance entirely.[45]

Opinion I want to be honest with you here the way I try to be in everything I write for this site. Earlier in this piece, before we started drafting, I told my collaborator I believed something like 90 percent of Americans oppose dissolving NATO. I was wrong on the specific number, and I want to own that rather than bury it. The actual data lands closer to 73 to 77 percent depending on which survey you look at, with only about 13 percent of Republicans supporting a full withdrawal. That is still an overwhelming majority. It is just not the number I guessed, and I would rather correct myself in print than let an inflated figure stand.

Opinion Here is what I think that real number tells us. There is a massive gap between what Americans across the political spectrum actually want from NATO and what a small number of officials, a president calling it a paper tiger, a former counterterrorism director warning we might leave specifically to back Israel against a NATO member, are floating as serious policy options. This is not a partisan disagreement playing out along the lines you would expect. Republican voters overwhelmingly want to stay. It is specific officials at the top, regardless of party, driving the conversation toward exit.

That gap between public opinion and official rhetoric is exactly what I want to close this piece out with in Part Nine and Part Ten, where I will lay out what is actually at stake if this rhetoric turns into policy, and where I stand on all of it.

Part Nine

What Is Actually at Stake

I have walked you through the rhetoric, the legal warrants, the NATO mechanics, and the polling. Now I want to lay out plainly what actually happens if this stops being rhetoric and becomes something real, because I do not think the scale of it has been made clear enough in most of the coverage I have read.

Start with the geography and infrastructure involved, because this is not an abstract alliance question. Turkey's Kurecik Radar Station plays a central role in NATO's ballistic missile defense architecture, providing early warning capabilities oriented specifically toward threats from Iran. NATO's Allied Land Command, based in Izmir, serves as the alliance's primary command for coordinating land forces, with additional facilities in Ankara, Batman, and Konya supporting defense coordination, drone operations, and AWACS deployments.[46] Turkey is not a peripheral NATO member. It sits at the operational center of the alliance's presence in the region that the “next Iran” rhetoric is about.

This is not hypothetical infrastructure sitting unused either. In March 2026, NATO expanded its temporary deployment of Patriot missile batteries, provided by the United States and Spain, across Turkish bases following heightened tensions with Iran.[47] Between March 4 and March 30 alone, NATO air and missile defense systems intercepted four separate ballistic missiles launched from Iran that entered or approached Turkish airspace.[48] American and allied systems, operating on Turkish soil, have already been actively defending Turkey during the exact period this rivalry with Israel has been escalating. That is the paradox I flagged back in Part One. The same alliance infrastructure protecting Turkey from Iran is the infrastructure Joe Kent is warning the US might abandon specifically to back Israel against Turkey.

Now layer in the regional alliance building on both sides, because this would not stay contained to just two countries. Reporting describes the worst case scenario as a new enemy axis consisting of Turkey and Qatar, joined by Pakistan and its nuclear capability. Steven Cook of the Council on Foreign Relations put it directly, warning that if Turkey succeeds in solidifying its relationship with Pakistan, the strategic map changes overnight, because at that point it is no longer just about the missiles coming from Iran, it becomes a question of a Sunni world with nuclear capability.[49] On the other side, Netanyahu's own hexagon alliance, built around Greece, Cyprus, India, and unnamed partners, is already forming in direct response to that same fear, as covered in Part Three.

This is where I think the stakes become concrete rather than abstract. If Turkey and Israel actually clash in Syria, the scenario Kent specifically described, you are not looking at a contained bilateral skirmish. You are looking at a NATO member with the alliance's second largest army, hosting critical missile defense infrastructure that has already intercepted Iranian missiles this year, going up against a country backed by American airpower, American intelligence, and now potentially a United States that has exited the alliance specifically to remove any obligation to sit on Turkey's side of that fight.

And remember, Netanyahu's own strikes have not stayed rhetorical. Iran went from warning to war. Hamas leadership in Doha went from stated target to airstrike. The Syrian base near the Turkish border went from warning to strike within days, the very event that opened this piece. If that pattern holds, the question is not whether tensions between Israel and Turkey stay at the level of arrest warrants and X posts. The question is what happens the first time Netanyahu decides Turkey has ignored a warning the way Syria did.

That is not a regional problem anymore at that point. That is Americans potentially watching a country we help arm and defend go to war with a country whose territory hosts American missile defense systems, American command infrastructure, and American troops, while the alliance meant to prevent exactly that kind of collision has already been discarded by the one country with the standing to stop it.

Part Ten

Who This Actually Serves

I want to close this the way I try to close every piece on this site, by asking who actually benefits from what I just spent nine sections documenting, because I think that question cuts through the noise faster than anything else.

Opinion Here is where I land after researching all of this. Netanyahu is not manufacturing a threat out of thin air. Turkey's regional ambitions are real, and I do not think Israeli concerns about Ankara's growing influence in Syria, Gaza, and its outreach toward Saudi Arabia and Pakistan are pure fiction. But I also do not think you can separate the timing of this rhetoric from Netanyahu's own re election fight, especially when a former Israeli ambassador is on record saying politicians like him rely on the perpetual threat of war to stay relevant. Both things can be true at once. The threat can be partly real and still be a political tool being sharpened for a specific electoral moment.

What I keep coming back to, though, is not Netanyahu or Erdogan. It is Joe Kent. A former director of the National Counterterrorism Center told us plainly that a US exit from NATO would not be about avoiding foreign entanglements, it would be about clearing the path to back Israel against a fellow NATO member. That is not a foreign national with an agenda. That is someone who sat inside our own government, who resigned specifically because he opposed the drift of American policy in this exact region, warning us in his own words what the real strategy is.

Opinion I think Americans are being asked to trade a defensive alliance that has kept the peace in Europe and the North Atlantic for eighty years for the privilege of picking a side in someone else's regional rivalry. And I do not think most Americans, myself included, ever agreed to that trade. The polling I laid out in Part Eight backs this up. Seventy three to seventy seven percent of Americans, depending on the survey, want to stay in NATO or increase our commitment to it. That includes a majority of Republicans and a majority of self identified MAGA Republicans. This is not a partisan divide where Democrats want in and Republicans want out. This is ordinary Americans across the political spectrum wanting to keep an alliance intact, while a small number of officials at the top float dismantling it for reasons that have nothing to do with protecting the American people.

This is exactly the kind of gap I started this publication to document. It is not left versus right. It is civilians versus the people making decisions on their behalf without their consent. Netanyahu answers to Israeli voters. Erdogan answers to Turkish voters. Neither of them owes the American public anything. But Joe Kent's warning was specifically about what our own government might do, using our own alliance, on our own dime, for a fight most Americans never asked to be part of.

Opinion I am not telling you Turkey is right and Israel is wrong, or the other way around. I have tried throughout this piece to hold both governments accountable for their own escalation, because that is what this site exists to do. What I am telling you is that if Turkey and Israel do end up clashing in Syria the way Kent described, and if the United States has already walked away from NATO by then, Americans will have lost a say in that outcome entirely, not because we voted for it, not because Congress debated it, but because a handful of officials decided our alliance was an obstacle rather than a protection. That should worry you regardless of how you feel about Netanyahu, Erdogan, or anyone else in this story. It worries me. And I think it should worry you too.
