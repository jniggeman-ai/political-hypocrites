The Escalator and the Swamp

Donald Trump's Political Career, 2015 to 2026 — The promises, the rise, the accusations, the comeback, and the collapse of the most powerful political coalition in modern American history

Introduction

The Pitch

On June 16, 2015, Donald Trump descended a gold escalator in the atrium of his Manhattan skyscraper and made a set of promises to the American people. He would build a wall across the southern border and make Mexico pay for it. He would eliminate the national debt in eight years. He would drain the Washington swamp of the corrupt insiders who had captured the government and were running it for themselves. He would not be beholden to donors, lobbyists, or special interests. He would put America first, and the people who had been left behind by the political establishment first within that.[1]

What followed over the next eleven years is the subject of this investigation. Not a prosecution, not a defense, and not a verdict delivered by the author. The record delivers its own verdict. This investigation reads that record in sequence: the promises made from the escalator, the campaign that followed, the first term and what it produced, the legal assault and the political exile, the comeback that redefined what American politics could look like, the second term and what it has done to every promise that preceded it, and the collapse of the most powerful political coalition a single American figure had assembled in living memory.

The story of Donald Trump's political career is, at its foundation, the story of a country that wanted to be told something true. Whether he told it that truth, or something that wore truth as a costume, is a question the documented record addresses in detail.

Part One

The Escalator

June 16, 2015

The announcement speech ran for approximately 45 minutes and covered more ground than any serious political observer gave it credit for at the time. Trump addressed trade, immigration, the national debt, veterans care, healthcare, foreign policy, and the corruption of the donor class in a single unscripted address. The coverage that day focused on the escalator, a paid crowd, and the most inflammatory line in the speech. Nearly all of it missed what the speech actually said.[2]

On immigration, Trump said the following: "When Mexico sends its people, they're not sending their best. They're bringing drugs. They're bringing crime. They're rapists. And some, I assume, are good people." The line was immediately seized upon by critics and would define media coverage of Trump for months. What received far less attention was the structural argument surrounding it: that the United States government had failed to enforce its own immigration laws for decades, that both parties bore responsibility for that failure, and that the political donor class had a direct financial interest in maintaining a pool of low-wage undocumented labor.[3]

On the national debt, Trump's promise was specific. "I will tell you, I will take care of it. I will pay off the debt. I will take care of it quickly." He later specified the timeline as eight years. The national debt on the day he spoke those words was approximately $18.1 trillion.[4]

Primary Source Document — Trump Announcement Speech, Trump Tower, New York, June 16, 2015

"I won't be running around the country begging people for money for my campaign. I won't owe anybody anything. I won't be beholden to anyone except to you, the American people, if you elect me to serve as your President."

On the donor class, the speech was unusually candid. Trump said politicians were controlled by the people who funded them, and that he was in a position to say so because he had been one of those funders. "I was the best," he said. "I give to everybody. When they call, I give. And you know what? When I need something from them, two years later, three years later, I call them, and they are there for me." It was a more direct description of how campaign finance corruption operates than any major party politician had offered in public in years.[5]

The press corps treated the speech as a sideshow. Political analysts assigned Trump single-digit odds of winning the Republican nomination. NBC cut business ties with him over the immigration remarks. The Republican establishment looked at the 17-person field it had assembled and concluded the newcomer would be gone by Labor Day.[6]

What the Pitch Was

The pitch had four load-bearing pillars. First, economic nationalism: the argument that trade agreements had hollowed out American manufacturing, that working-class wages had stagnated as a result, and that both parties had enabled this because their donors benefited from it. Second, immigration enforcement: the argument that the federal government had deliberately failed to enforce existing law. Third, anti-corruption: the argument that Washington was run by a donor class that purchased political outcomes, that this was a bipartisan problem, and that only a self-funded outsider could break the cycle. Fourth, a promise of competence: the argument that the country's problems were not complicated, just corrupted, and that a businessman who had actually run things could solve them.

There is one more thing that needs to be said about the escalator speech before this investigation moves forward. The promises Trump made that day were not new ideas. The argument that Washington was captured by donors had been made by progressives for decades. The argument that trade agreements had gutted manufacturing had been made by labor unions and by economists on the left and right for just as long. The argument that immigration enforcement had been deliberately neglected had been made by politicians in both parties. What was new was not the diagnosis. What was new was the man willing to say all of it at once, on a gold escalator, in front of television cameras, with no apparent concern for the consequences.

$18.1T

The U.S. national debt on June 16, 2015, the day Trump promised to eliminate it within eight years. By the end of his first term it had grown to $26.9 trillion. The debt crossed $39 trillion in early 2026.

Part Two

The Primary

The Field

The 2016 Republican primary field was, by any historical measure, an impressive collection of political talent. It included two sitting governors with significant executive records, a former governor who had won reelection in a swing state by 30 points, a sitting senator who had been his party's vice-presidential nominee four years earlier, two additional sitting senators, a former CEO, a retired neurosurgeon, and a former governor and cabinet secretary. The Republican National Committee had spent the years since 2012 preparing what it called an "autopsy" of its previous losses, and the conclusion of that autopsy was that the party needed to broaden its coalition, moderate its tone on immigration, and improve its ground game operation.

Donald Trump invalidated every assumption of that autopsy within six months.

The Method

Trump's primary strategy, to the extent it can be called a strategy rather than an instinct, rested on a few recognizable mechanics. He dominated earned media at a scale that had no precedent in primary politics. A 2016 study by the research firm mediaQuant calculated that Trump had accumulated approximately $1.9 billion in earned media value before a single primary vote was cast, a figure that exceeded the comparable earned media value of every other candidate in the field combined. He did not need to buy airtime because the networks gave it to him. He was not given airtime because anyone in television news thought he would win. He was given airtime because he produced ratings.

The debates illustrated the dynamic precisely. Trump entered each debate as the gravitational center of the stage. He assigned nicknames to opponents that reduced complex political figures to single phrases. Jeb Bush became "Low Energy Jeb." Marco Rubio became "Little Marco." Ted Cruz became "Lyin' Ted." These were not policy arguments. They were not intended to be. They were designed to define the opponent before the opponent could define himself. Trump did it in three words, in front of an audience of 20 million people, for free.

What the nicknames also did was signal to a specific kind of voter that Trump was not playing the game everyone else was playing. The Republican establishment's implicit social contract with its primary voters was that a certain level of decorum would be maintained. Trump broke that contract in the first debate and never looked back. For voters who had spent years watching polished politicians deliver carefully managed messages and then do nothing they had promised, the spectacle of a man refusing to perform the expected rituals read as authenticity.

The Opponents Fall

Scott Walker, the Wisconsin governor who had broken public sector unions and survived a recall election and was considered by many Republican insiders to be the likely nominee, dropped out of the race in September 2015, three months after Trump entered. He explicitly cited Trump's dominance of the media environment as a factor.

Jeb Bush, who had entered the race with more than $100 million in super PAC money and the full support of the Republican donor network, spent approximately $130 million on his campaign and received four delegates before suspending in February 2016. The spectacle of a candidate with limitless establishment resources being dismantled by a man who had called him low energy in a debate became one of the defining images of the entire election cycle.

Marco Rubio made the catastrophic decision to attempt to beat Trump at his own game during a February debate, mocking Trump's spray tan and the size of his hands. Rubio lost his home state of Florida to Trump by nearly 20 points on March 15 and suspended his campaign that night.

Ted Cruz lasted the longest, winning 11 states and accumulating enough delegates to raise the possibility of a contested convention. Cruz refused to endorse Trump at the Republican National Convention in July 2016, was booed off the stage, and two years later was campaigning for Trump in Texas.

What the Primary Revealed

By the time the last primary votes were cast, Trump had received more primary votes than any Republican candidate in the history of the party. He had done it without a traditional ground game, without a conventional digital operation, without the endorsement of a single major Republican newspaper, and while being opposed at various points by the sitting Republican Speaker of the House, the Republican Senate majority leader, the last two Republican presidential nominees, and essentially the entire apparatus of the conservative media establishment.

What the primary revealed, for anyone willing to read it honestly, was that the Republican base and the Republican establishment had not been the same thing for a very long time. The base had been voting for promises that the establishment had no intention of keeping. Trade deals kept getting signed. Immigration enforcement kept not happening. The debt kept growing. The swamp kept filling. Trump did not create that disconnect. He found it, named it, and rode it to the nomination.

Whether he intended to fix it, or whether he simply intended to exploit it, is the question the rest of this investigation addresses.

Part Three

The First Campaign vs. Clinton

The General Election Landscape

When Donald Trump accepted the Republican nomination in Cleveland on July 21, 2016, virtually every major forecasting model in American political science gave him a significant disadvantage against Hillary Clinton. The electoral map favored Democrats. Clinton had a fully operational national campaign infrastructure, the unified support of the Democratic Party, the implicit endorsement of a sitting president with a 50 percent approval rating, and the backing of nearly every major institutional voice in American public life.

She also had thirty years of accumulated political baggage, a federal investigation into her use of a private email server while serving as Secretary of State, and a favorability rating that was, by the standards of any previous major party nominee, historically poor. The 2016 general election was a contest between two of the least popular major party nominees in the history of modern polling.

The Email Investigation and the Comey Letter

In July 2016, FBI Director James Comey held an unusual press conference announcing that the bureau was recommending no criminal charges against Clinton, while simultaneously describing her handling of classified information as "extremely careless." Then, on October 28, 2016, eleven days before the election, Comey sent a letter to Congress informing members that the FBI had discovered emails potentially relevant to the Clinton investigation on a laptop belonging to Anthony Weiner, the estranged husband of Clinton aide Huma Abedin. Clinton's polling numbers, which had been moving in her direction following the final presidential debate, reversed immediately.[7]

On November 6, two days before the election, Comey sent a second letter saying the review was complete and the original conclusion stood. The damage was done. A study published in the journal Political Research Quarterly found the letter cost Clinton approximately 2 percentage points in the national popular vote, a swing more than sufficient to explain the Electoral College outcome.[8]

Russian Interference

On January 6, 2017, the United States Intelligence Community released a declassified assessment concluding that Russian President Vladimir Putin had ordered an influence campaign aimed at the 2016 presidential election designed to harm Clinton's candidacy and help Trump's. The CIA and FBI assessed this with high confidence; the NSA assessed it with moderate confidence. All three agencies agreed on the core conclusion.[9]

The Senate Intelligence Committee's subsequent five-volume report, completed in 2020, confirmed and significantly expanded the intelligence community's findings. It documented extensive contacts between Trump campaign officials and individuals connected to Russian intelligence, and concluded that campaign chairman Paul Manafort's sharing of internal polling data with a Russian associate with ties to Russian intelligence represented a grave counterintelligence threat. It did not conclude that the Trump campaign had directed or coordinated the Russian interference operation.[10]

The Result

On November 8, 2016, Donald Trump won the presidency of the United States. He carried 306 electoral votes to Clinton's 232. He won Pennsylvania by 44,292 votes, Michigan by 10,704 votes, and Wisconsin by 22,748 votes. Those three states, with a combined margin of approximately 77,000 votes out of more than 136 million cast nationally, delivered the Electoral College majority.[11]

Clinton received approximately 2.9 million more votes nationally. Trump became the fifth president in American history to win the Electoral College while losing the popular vote.

The Republican establishment that had spent a year trying to stop him began, within weeks, the process of pretending it had always been for him.

What He Promised in the Closing Weeks

In the final stretch of the campaign, Trump's closing argument crystallized the promises he would be held to. He would repeal and replace the Affordable Care Act on day one. He would immediately begin construction of the wall. He would label China a currency manipulator on his first day in office. He would bring back manufacturing jobs by renegotiating trade deals. He would not cut Social Security, Medicare, or Medicaid. He would eliminate the deficit and begin paying down the national debt.

These were not vague aspirations. They were specific, dated, and documented commitments. This investigation returns to each of them in Part Four.

Part Four

The First Term: Promises vs. Record, 2017 to 2021

The Trump presidency began on January 20, 2017, with an inaugural address that described "American carnage," spoke of "rusted out factories scattered like tombstones across the landscape," and framed his inauguration explicitly as the moment when power was being returned from the Washington establishment to the people. What followed over the next four years was a presidency that, measured against its own stated promises, produced a record far more complicated than either its supporters or its critics have generally been willing to acknowledge.

The Wall

Over four years, the Trump administration constructed approximately 450 miles of border barrier. Of those 450 miles, the overwhelming majority replaced or reinforced existing fencing and infrastructure. The administration's own Customs and Border Protection records documented that approximately 80 miles represented new barrier in locations where no previous structure had existed.[12]

Mexico did not pay for any of it. The administration ultimately redirected approximately $15 billion in Pentagon funds to barrier construction after declaring a national emergency. The GAO found the administration had violated the Impoundment Control Act in the process.[13]

The National Debt

The national debt on inauguration day 2017 was approximately $19.9 trillion. By the end of Trump's first term, it had grown to approximately $26.9 trillion, an increase of $7 trillion in four years. This was, in raw dollar terms, the largest single-term increase in the national debt in American history at that point.[14]

The increase had two primary drivers. The first was the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, which the Joint Committee on Taxation estimated would reduce federal revenues by approximately $1.5 trillion over ten years. The second driver was COVID-19 relief spending beginning in 2020, which added approximately $3.1 trillion to the debt in a single year. The debt trajectory before the pandemic was already moving sharply upward.[15]

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, signed into law in December 2017, permanently reduced the corporate tax rate from 35 percent to 21 percent and temporarily reduced individual income tax rates across all brackets. The nonpartisan Tax Policy Center found that the largest benefits, measured as a percentage of after-tax income, flowed disproportionately to higher-income households and to corporations. The corporate rate cut was permanent. The individual cuts were temporary and set to expire in 2025. The act also eliminated the individual mandate of the Affordable Care Act, which the CBO initially projected would result in 13 million fewer Americans with health insurance over the following decade, a figure subsequently revised as enrollment behavior differed from the model.[16]

Healthcare: The Repeal That Wasn't

Trump had promised to repeal and replace the Affordable Care Act on day one. The Senate voted on multiple replacement proposals in July 2017. All of them failed. The decisive vote came on July 28, when Senator John McCain walked onto the Senate floor in the early hours of the morning and cast a thumbs-down vote against the so-called skinny repeal bill, killing the effort entirely. McCain was battling brain cancer and died thirteen months later. Trump attacked him publicly before and after his death. The ACA remained law.

The Swamp: Stocked, Not Drained

Rex Tillerson, his first Secretary of State, was the former CEO of ExxonMobil. Scott Pruitt, his first EPA Administrator, was eventually forced to resign amid multiple federal investigations into ethics violations. Ryan Zinke, his Interior Secretary, resigned under investigation. Tom Price, his first HHS Secretary, resigned after revelations about his use of private charter flights at taxpayer expense. Betsy DeVos, his Education Secretary, was a billionaire donor with no background in public education. Steven Mnuchin, his Treasury Secretary, was a Goldman Sachs veteran.[17]

By the time Trump left office, his administration had seen more cabinet-level departures for ethics-related reasons than any administration in the modern era. Trump had not drained the swamp. He had changed its tenants.

Trade

On trade, Trump's record was the most substantively consistent with his campaign promises. He withdrew the United States from the Trans-Pacific Partnership on his fourth day in office. He renegotiated NAFTA into the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, which trade economists described as a modest update. He launched a trade war with China, imposing tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods. China responded with retaliatory tariffs on American agricultural products. The administration responded with approximately $28 billion in direct subsidy payments to American farmers. The trade deficit with China, which Trump had promised to eliminate, did not significantly decrease during his first term.[18]

What the First Term Was

The first term produced three confirmed Supreme Court justices who reshaped the court for a generation: Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh after a nationally traumatic confirmation process, and Amy Coney Barrett, confirmed eight days before the 2020 election after Republicans had refused to hold hearings for Obama nominee Merrick Garland in the final year of his presidency on the stated principle that Supreme Court vacancies should not be filled in election years.

It produced significant deregulation across the energy, financial, and environmental sectors. It produced an economy that, through early 2020, performed well on conventional metrics, with low unemployment and rising wages, though economists debate how much of that performance was attributable to Trump's policies versus the continuation of trends already in motion when he took office. And then the pandemic arrived.

Part Five

The Accusations: First Term

Russiagate

The investigation conducted by Special Counsel Robert Mueller became the defining legal and political event of the first term. It is also one of the most systematically misrepresented investigations in modern American political history, misrepresented by both sides, in opposite directions, with equal confidence.

The Special Counsel's Office filed charges against 34 individuals and three companies. The charges included conspiracy against the United States, wire fraud, bank fraud, tax fraud, lying to federal investigators, and witness tampering. Trump's campaign chairman Paul Manafort was convicted on eight counts of financial fraud and later pleaded guilty to conspiracy charges. Trump's first national security advisor Michael Flynn pleaded guilty to lying to the FBI. Trump's personal attorney Michael Cohen pleaded guilty to multiple charges including campaign finance violations connected to hush money payments. Trump's longtime political advisor Roger Stone was convicted on seven counts including witness tampering and lying to Congress. Trump's foreign policy advisor George Papadopoulos pleaded guilty to lying to the FBI about his contacts with individuals connected to Russian intelligence.[19]

On the central question of whether the Trump campaign conspired or coordinated with the Russian government's interference operation, Volume I of the Mueller report concluded that the investigation "did not establish that members of the Trump campaign conspired or coordinated with Russia in its election interference activities." That sentence has been quoted accurately and frequently by Trump and his supporters. What has been quoted far less frequently is what surrounds it in the same document.

The report documented that the Trump campaign welcomed the Russian interference and expected to benefit from it. It documented that when Donald Trump Jr. received an email offering "dirt" on Hillary Clinton as part of what the email described as the Russian government's support for his father's campaign, he replied: "If it's what you say I love it especially later in the summer." He then attended the resulting meeting at Trump Tower along with Manafort and Jared Kushner.[20]

Obstruction

Volume II of the Mueller report documented ten separate episodes in which Trump potentially obstructed justice. They included his firing of FBI Director James Comey in May 2017, his repeated attempts to have Mueller removed, his direction of White House Counsel Don McGahn to have Mueller fired and then to deny having done so, his attempts to limit the scope of the investigation, his dangling of pardons to witnesses, and his public and private attacks on cooperating witnesses.[21]

Mueller did not conclude that Trump obstructed justice. He also explicitly did not exonerate him. The report stated that the Special Counsel's Office operated under a Department of Justice policy that a sitting president could not be indicted, and that the decision not to conclude obstruction had occurred was a legal and procedural judgment, not an evidentiary one. Attorney General William Barr's four-page summary of the 448-page report subsequently stated the report found no collusion and no obstruction. Mueller wrote to Barr saying the summary did not "fully capture the context, nature, and substance" of the report's conclusions. A federal judge later found that Barr had "distorted" the report's findings.[22]

The First Impeachment: Ukraine

On July 25, 2019, Trump placed a phone call to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky. The White House subsequently released a reconstructed transcript of the call. The transcript showed Trump asking Zelensky, whose country was dependent on American military aid in its conflict with Russian-backed separatists, to investigate two matters: the activities of a Ukrainian energy company called Burisma, on whose board Hunter Biden had served, and a debunked theory that Ukraine rather than Russia had interfered in the 2016 election. Trump and the Trump campaign had no documented involvement with Burisma. The request was an attempt to generate damaging information about Joe Biden, who was at that point the leading Democratic candidate for president.[23]

The underlying allegation that Biden as Vice President had pressured Ukraine to fire a prosecutor to protect his son's business interests was investigated and not substantiated. The prosecutor in question, Viktor Shokin, was widely regarded by U.S. officials, European allies, and anti-corruption advocates as corrupt and ineffective. His removal was official U.S. and European Union policy, not a personal favor to the Biden family.

Ambassador Gordon Sondland, a Trump donor appointed to his position, testified before the House Intelligence Committee that there was a clear "quid pro quo." The House voted to impeach Trump on December 18, 2019, on two articles: abuse of power and obstruction of Congress. Senator Mitt Romney became the first senator in American history to vote to convict a president of his own party. Every other Republican senator voted to acquit.[24]

What the First Term's Legal Record Shows

By the end of the first term, six Trump associates had been convicted or had pleaded guilty to federal crimes. The FBI's handling of the Russia investigation did include documented procedural abuses, a point the Department of Justice Inspector General's report confirmed in December 2019, finding significant errors and omissions in the FISA warrant applications used to surveil Trump campaign associate Carter Page. Those abuses were real, documented, and deserve to be named as such. They do not change what the Mueller report found. Both things are true simultaneously.

Part Six

COVID and the End of the First Term

The Pandemic Arrives

On January 9, 2020, the World Health Organization reported that Chinese authorities had identified a cluster of pneumonia cases in Wuhan with an unknown cause. On January 20, the first confirmed case of what would become COVID-19 was identified in the United States. On January 31, the Trump administration declared a public health emergency and announced restrictions on travel from China.[25]

The Early Response

In February 2020, Trump made a series of public statements that downplayed the severity of the emerging outbreak. On February 27, he said: "It's going to disappear. One day, it's like a miracle, it will disappear." In September 2020, journalist Bob Woodward published recordings of interviews with Trump conducted in February of that year. In those recordings, Trump told Woodward on February 7 that the virus was deadly, that it was more deadly than the flu, and that it was transmitted through the air. Trump had understood the severity of the virus at the moment he was publicly minimizing it. Asked why he had downplayed it, Trump told Woodward he did not want to create a panic.[26]

The Federal Response

Operation Warp Speed, the public-private partnership to accelerate COVID-19 vaccine development, was an extraordinary logistical and scientific accomplishment. Two vaccines received emergency use authorization before the end of 2020, less than a year after the virus was identified. The administration also negotiated the CARES Act, a $2.2 trillion relief package that passed with overwhelming bipartisan support in March 2020.[27]

The federal government's handling of the early testing rollout was a documented disaster. The CDC produced a flawed initial test that had to be recalled. By the time community spread was confirmed, the United States had conducted far fewer tests per capita than comparable nations. Trump publicly contradicted CDC Director Robert Redfield and Dr. Anthony Fauci on multiple occasions. By the time Trump left office in January 2021, approximately 400,000 Americans had died of COVID-19.[28]

January 6

On November 3, 2020, Joe Biden defeated Donald Trump in the presidential election. Biden received 81 million votes, the most ever cast for a presidential candidate in American history. Trump received 74 million votes, the most ever cast for a losing presidential candidate. Biden won the Electoral College 306 to 232.

Trump did not concede. Beginning on election night and continuing for the next two months, Trump and his allies advanced the claim that the election had been stolen through widespread fraud. They filed 62 lawsuits in state and federal courts across the contested states. They lost 61 of them. Attorney General William Barr told the Associated Press in December 2020 that the DOJ had found no evidence of fraud sufficient to change the outcome of the election.[29]

On January 6, 2021, Trump held a rally on the Ellipse near the White House at which he told a crowd of thousands that the election had been stolen, that they needed to fight like hell, and that they should march to the Capitol. He said he would march with them. He did not march with them. He returned to the White House and, according to extensive testimony before the House Select Committee, watched television coverage of what followed for hours without taking action to stop it.[30]

What followed was the most serious breach of the United States Capitol since the British burned it in 1814. Rioters overwhelmed Capitol Police, broke through doors and windows, occupied the Senate chamber, and forced members of Congress and the Vice President to shelter in place or evacuate. Five people died in direct connection with the events of that day. Approximately 140 police officers were injured.

The Second Impeachment

On January 13, 2021, the House impeached Trump for the second time on a single article of incitement of insurrection. The vote was 232 to 197. Ten Republican members voted in favor. The Senate trial resulted in a 57 to 43 vote to convict, short of the two-thirds majority required. Trump was acquitted for the second time.

Senator Mitch McConnell, who had voted to acquit on the constitutional jurisdictional question, gave a floor speech immediately after stating that Trump was "practically and morally responsible" for the events of January 6 and that "there is no question that President Trump is practically and morally responsible for provoking the events of that day." McConnell subsequently endorsed Trump for the 2024 presidential election.[31]

Part Seven

The Exile and the Lawsuits

The Wilderness

Donald Trump left Washington on the morning of January 20, 2021, without attending Joe Biden's inauguration, the first president to skip his successor's swearing-in since Andrew Johnson in 1869. By August 2023, a former president of the United States was facing 91 felony counts across four separate criminal cases in four different jurisdictions.

The New York Hush Money Case

Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg indicted Trump on March 30, 2023, on 34 counts of falsifying business records in the first degree. The indictment centered on a $130,000 payment made by Trump's then-attorney Michael Cohen to adult film actress Stormy Daniels in exchange for her agreement not to publicly discuss a sexual encounter she alleged had occurred in 2006. Trump has denied the encounter took place.[32]

The jury convicted Trump on all 34 counts on May 30, 2024. He became the first former president of the United States to be convicted of felony crimes. Sentencing resulted in an unconditional discharge, meaning Trump received no jail time, no probation, and no fine. Judge Juan Merchan cited the unprecedented nature of sentencing a president-elect as a significant factor.[33]

The Classified Documents Case

Special Counsel Jack Smith indicted Trump on June 9, 2023, in the Southern District of Florida on 37 counts related to his retention of classified documents at Mar-a-Lago after leaving office. A superseding indictment brought the total to 40 counts. Surveillance footage showed boxes being moved in the facility after a subpoena for their return had been received. An audio recording captured Trump apparently showing a classified document to individuals who did not have security clearances while acknowledging he had not declassified it. The case was dismissed in November 2024 following Trump's election victory, on the grounds that DOJ policy prohibited the prosecution of a sitting president.[34]

The January 6 Federal Case

Special Counsel Jack Smith filed a separate indictment against Trump on August 1, 2023, charging him with four counts related to the effort to overturn the 2020 election results. In Trump v. United States, decided in July 2024, the Supreme Court ruled six to three that former presidents enjoy broad immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts taken while in office. The case was dismissed in November 2024.[35]

The Georgia Case

Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis indicted Trump and 18 co-defendants on August 14, 2023, under Georgia's RICO statute on 41 counts. Among the specific allegations was a recorded phone call in which Trump asked Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger to "find" 11,780 votes, exactly one more than Biden's margin of victory in the state. Raffensperger refused. The case collapsed following Trump's election victory. Judge Scott McAfee dismissed the charges against Trump in November 2025.[36]

The Civil Cases

In May 2023, a federal jury found Trump liable for sexually abusing writer E. Jean Carroll and for defaming her when he denied it, awarding Carroll $5 million in damages. In a subsequent defamation trial in January 2024, the jury awarded Carroll an additional $83.3 million in damages. In February 2024, New York Judge Arthur Engoron issued a civil fraud judgment requiring Trump to pay approximately $364 million in penalties and interest for systematically inflating his assets in financial statements used to obtain loans and insurance.[37]

The Political Arithmetic of the Lawsuits

The four criminal cases, the civil judgments, and the relentless legal proceedings of 2023 and 2024 did something that none of Trump's political opponents had managed to do: they made him more popular within the Republican Party. Every indictment was followed by a surge in his poll numbers among Republican primary voters. The argument that the legal system was being weaponized against a political opponent resonated with a base that had been primed by years of documented institutional failures to distrust the apparatus being used against him. The attempt to hold Trump legally accountable for his conduct accelerated his path back to the presidency.

Part Eight

The Second Campaign

The Primary That Was Not a Contest

Trump announced his candidacy for the 2024 presidential election on November 15, 2022. The Republican primary field that assembled to challenge him was, on paper, formidable. Ron DeSantis entered the race in May 2023 with significant donor support. Nikki Haley ran a disciplined and competent campaign. None of it mattered. Trump's grip on the Republican base was, if anything, tighter than it had been in 2016. The indictments had consolidated Republican voters around him.

Trump won the Iowa caucuses on January 15, 2024, with 51 percent of the vote, the largest margin in the history of the Iowa caucuses. Nikki Haley suspended her campaign on March 6 without endorsing Trump. He accepted the Republican nomination in July 2024 in Milwaukee, the first convicted felon to be nominated for president by a major American political party.

Biden's Collapse

Joe Biden had been showing signs of significant cognitive decline that were visible to anyone paying close attention for at least two years before the 2024 election. The White House and the Democratic Party dismissed all of it as right-wing disinformation.

Then came June 27, 2024. CNN hosted the first presidential debate of the general election season. Biden appeared confused, lost his train of thought repeatedly, spoke in a barely audible voice at moments, and was unable to complete several answers coherently. Trump, whatever one thinks of his conduct and his record, was sharp, aggressive, and in command of the stage.[38]

Biden announced his withdrawal from the race on July 21, 2024, twenty-four days after the debate. He endorsed Vice President Kamala Harris. The Democratic National Committee conducted an accelerated nomination process that resulted in Harris becoming the nominee without a contested primary.

The Nomination That Was Not a Nomination

Harris had not competed in a single primary. She had received zero primary votes. The Democratic Party, which had spent years arguing that democracy itself was under assault from Donald Trump, selected its presidential nominee through an internal process involving party insiders, donor calls, and pressure campaigns on elected officials rather than through a vote of its own members. No other candidate was seriously considered. No debate among potential nominees was held.

Whether the process was the right call given the circumstances is a legitimate debate. What is not debatable is what it was. It was an appointment, not a nomination in any meaningful democratic sense. A party that had made the protection of democratic norms the centerpiece of its anti-Trump argument chose its standard-bearer through a process that had no democratic accountability whatsoever. That contradiction was noted at the time by voices on the left as well as the right.

The Coalition That Came Back Larger

Trump made significant inroads with Black and Hispanic voters compared to his previous campaigns, particularly among men without college degrees. He won a majority of Hispanic men according to most exit polls. He won voters who cited the economy as their primary concern by a substantial margin. The Bronx gave Trump his highest vote share there since 1988. South Texas counties along the Rio Grande that had been Democratic for generations moved sharply in his direction.[39]

Trump won the 2024 election with 312 electoral votes to Harris's 226. He won the popular vote with approximately 49.8 percent to Harris's 48.3 percent, becoming the first Republican to win the national popular vote since George W. Bush in 2004. He swept all seven battleground states.

Part Nine

The Second Term Begins

January 20, 2025

Donald Trump was inaugurated for the second time on January 20, 2025, inside the Capitol Rotunda. The guests at the inauguration included Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, Sundar Pichai, and other technology billionaires in prominent positions in the front rows. The image of the richest men in the world gathered to celebrate the inauguration of the man who had promised to drain the swamp and give power back to the people was noted by commentators across the political spectrum.

Trump signed a cascade of executive orders in the hours following his inauguration. He pardoned or commuted the sentences of approximately 1,500 individuals convicted in connection with the January 6 Capitol breach, including those convicted of violent offenses against police officers. He declared a national emergency at the southern border. He withdrew the United States from the Paris Climate Agreement for the second time. He withdrew from the World Health Organization. He ended birthright citizenship by executive order, immediately challenged in federal courts.[40]

DOGE

The Department of Government Efficiency was not a government department. It had no statutory authority, no congressional authorization, and no formal legal standing as a government entity. It was a private advisory initiative led by Elon Musk, who had spent approximately $250 million supporting Trump's 2024 campaign and related political efforts.

The promise attached to DOGE was staggering in its ambition. Musk initially said DOGE would cut $2 trillion from the federal budget. That figure was later revised to $1 trillion. That figure was later revised to $150 billion. The CATO Institute found that federal outlays in the first eleven months of fiscal year 2025 totaled approximately $7.6 trillion, approximately $248 billion higher than the same period in the prior year. Politico found that of the $145 billion in contract cancellations DOGE claimed through June 2025, independent analysts could verify approximately $1.4 billion in actual cash savings.[41]

The Tariffs

Trump imposed sweeping tariffs in the early months of his second term: a 10 percent baseline tariff on all imports, a 25 percent tariff on goods from Canada and Mexico, and tariffs on Chinese goods that in some categories reached 145 percent. Consumer prices on tariffed goods rose. The agricultural sector faced retaliatory tariffs from trading partners. The tariffs functioned as a consumption tax paid disproportionately by lower and middle income households, who spend a higher share of their income on goods than wealthy households do.[42]

The One Big Beautiful Bill

The budget reconciliation bill passed the House on May 22, 2025, passed the Senate on July 1, 2025, and was signed into law on July 4, 2025. It extended the expiring provisions of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, added new tax cuts including the elimination of taxes on tips and overtime pay, increased defense spending, and made substantial cuts to Medicaid, food stamps, and student loan programs.

The Congressional Budget Office scored the enacted bill as adding approximately $3.4 trillion to the national debt over the 2025 to 2034 period. The CBO's February 2026 baseline projected that total deficits over the 2026 to 2035 period would be $1.4 trillion larger than prior projections, and that the national debt would reach 120 percent of GDP within the decade.[43]

Trump had promised on the campaign trail that he would not cut Medicaid, Medicare, or Social Security. The One Big Beautiful Bill cut Medicaid by approximately $800 billion over ten years according to the CBO, the largest single cut to the program in its history.

The Cabinet

Matt Gaetz was nominated as Attorney General. Gaetz had been under House Ethics Committee investigation for alleged sexual misconduct, illicit drug use, and sex trafficking. He withdrew his nomination before the Ethics Committee report on his conduct was released. Pete Hegseth, a Fox News host with no senior military or civilian defense leadership experience, was confirmed as Secretary of Defense. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., whose public statements on public health were at odds with the scientific consensus on multiple issues, was confirmed as Secretary of Health and Human Services.[44]

Part Ten

The Swamp Takes the Throne

The Meme Coins

Two days before his inauguration, on January 18, 2025, Trump launched a personal cryptocurrency called $TRUMP through his official social media accounts. Within days it had reached a market capitalization of approximately $15 billion. Approximately 80 percent of the coin's supply was controlled by entities connected to Trump and his affiliated companies. Two days later, on inauguration day itself, Melania Trump launched a separate cryptocurrency called $MELANIA. Both coins dropped sharply in value after their initial peaks, with many retail investors suffering significant losses.[45]

Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representative Jake Auchincloss wrote to the Office of Government Ethics, the Treasury, the SEC, and the CFTC on January 23, 2025, asking for an investigation into whether the coins constituted corruption or securities violations. Former House Ethics Committee Chairman Charles Dent, a Republican, stated publicly that the coins represented an unprecedented conflict of interest.[46]

The Crypto Dinner

In May 2025, the Trump organization announced an exclusive dinner at the White House for the top holders of the $TRUMP meme coin. The top 220 holders of the coin were invited. The practical effect was that purchasing large quantities of Trump's personal cryptocurrency was the mechanism for obtaining private access to the President of the United States. Foreign nationals were not explicitly excluded from participation. Representative Adam Smith and Representative Sean Casten wrote to the Department of Justice requesting a criminal investigation into whether the dinner constituted bribery or an illegal gratuity.[47]

DOGE and the Conflict of Interest at Its Center

Elon Musk, the world's wealthiest person, had been given effective authority to review, restructure, and in many cases terminate federal contracts and agencies. Musk's companies, including SpaceX, Tesla, Starlink, and others, had billions of dollars in existing federal contracts and pending regulatory matters before the very agencies DOGE was restructuring. Musk had not divested from these companies. He had not recused himself from matters touching them.[48]

The Tariff Exemptions

ProPublica and other investigative outlets documented that companies with lobbyists who had personal relationships with administration officials, companies that had made significant contributions to Trump-aligned political organizations, and companies whose executives had attended Trump fundraising events received exemptions at substantially higher rates than similarly situated companies without those connections.[49]

What This Was

What distinguished the second Trump term was the elimination of the intermediary layer. Previous administrations maintained at least the formal apparatus of a separation between the president's personal financial interests and the exercise of executive power. The meme coins, the crypto dinner, the DOGE structure, and the tariff exemption process represented the effective removal of that separation. The president's personal financial instruments became mechanisms for purchasing access to the president. The man who had promised that he alone could fix the corruption of Washington had constructed, in plain sight, a system that made the corruption he had promised to end look modest by comparison.

This is not a partisan observation. It was made, in those terms or close to them, by Republican former officials, conservative legal scholars, libertarian policy analysts, and the government's own contracting and ethics records.

Part Eleven

The Coalition in Freefall

The Numbers

Trump entered his second term with an approval rating of approximately 47 percent, the highest of his political career. By mid-2026, that approval rating had fallen to a range of 37 to 40 percent depending on the pollster, a decline of seven to ten points in approximately eighteen months. The drop was not driven primarily by Democrats. It was driven by independents and by a measurable erosion among Republicans and Republican-leaning voters.[50]

The Broken Promises, Itemized

The national debt had not decreased. It had accelerated. Medicaid had been cut by approximately $800 billion over ten years under the One Big Beautiful Bill. Trump had promised explicitly and repeatedly that he would not cut Medicaid. The wars had not ended in 24 hours. Ukraine was still at war. The Middle East was not at peace. Trump had bombed Iranian nuclear sites in June 2025. The swamp had not been drained. DOGE had not cut $2 trillion, or $1 trillion, or $150 billion.

The Epstein Files

Attorney General Pam Bondi appeared on Fox News in February 2025 and was asked about an Epstein client list. She replied that it was "sitting on my desk right now for review." On July 7, 2025, the Department of Justice and FBI released a two-page memo summarizing their exhaustive review of all Epstein files in the government's possession. The memo concluded: there was no incriminating client list. There was no credible evidence that Epstein had blackmailed prominent individuals. There was no evidence that could predicate an investigation against any uncharged third parties. Epstein had died by suicide. No further disclosure was appropriate or warranted.[51]

A Democracy Institute poll released July 15, 2025 found that 67 percent of Trump's own 2024 voters disagreed with his administration's handling of the Epstein files. Sixty-three percent of Americans overall disapproved. Fifty-six percent of respondents in a separate Rasmussen survey said they did not believe the DOJ and FBI were telling the truth.[52]

Lauren Boebert publicly called for a special counsel to be appointed to oversee the Epstein matter. Marjorie Taylor Greene confronted Trump directly about the Epstein files and resigned from the House of Representatives, with the announcement made on November 21, 2025, effective January 5, 2026, citing her disillusionment with Trump and the direction he was taking the MAGA movement. Trump responded on Truth Social by calling her "Marjorie 'Traitor' Brown." Elon Musk publicly called the DOJ memo's conclusions a turning point in his relationship with the administration.[53]

Opinion

The Epstein episode revealed something about the MAGA coalition that its critics had consistently underestimated and its boosters had consistently ignored. The coalition was not, at its core, simply a personality cult. It was built on a genuine and documented grievance: that powerful people operate by different rules, that institutions protect them, and that the official account of events involving the rich and connected is not reliable. That grievance is not paranoid. It is supported by an extensive evidentiary record across multiple decades and institutions. Trump had understood that grievance better than anyone in American politics and had channeled it more effectively than anyone before him. When his own DOJ produced a two-page memo saying there was nothing there and closed the file, a significant portion of his coalition concluded, not unreasonably, that the man they had trusted to expose the machine had either been absorbed by it or had never intended to dismantle it in the first place.

The Musk Divorce

Musk had spent approximately $250 million supporting Trump's 2024 campaign. He had been given an unprecedented role in the executive branch. Over the course of roughly six months, the relationship collapsed in public. The specific triggers were multiple: the One Big Beautiful Bill, which Musk opposed as fiscally irresponsible and which passed without his input; the Epstein memo; foreign policy decisions that contradicted the isolationist commitments of the campaign. A Musk who had spent 2024 making Trump the centerpiece of his political messaging was now making the argument, to the same audience, that Trump had failed.

The Midterm Map

The 2026 midterm elections were approaching against a backdrop of a president whose approval rating had fallen ten points from his inauguration peak, a base with documented fractures over Epstein, DOGE's unfulfilled promises, Medicaid cuts that directly affected working-class households, and a national debt trajectory that contradicted every fiscal promise Trump had ever made. The historical pattern for midterm elections is well established: the party holding the White House loses seats in Congress in the president's first midterm almost without exception. A president at 37 to 40 percent approval faces a structural environment in which significant losses are the baseline expectation.

Part Twelve

The Architecture of the Collapse

The Men Who Were Removed

To understand why the MAGA coalition fractured as quickly and as deeply as it did, you have to understand what it lost before the fracturing began. Political movements do not collapse only because of what their leaders do wrong. They collapse because the people who held them together are no longer there to hold them. By the time the second term's failures became undeniable, two of the three most important non-Trump figures in the entire MAGA ecosystem were gone. One had been forced out. One had been killed.

Tucker Carlson: The Voice That Built the Church

Tucker Carlson was fired from Fox News on April 24, 2023, in circumstances that were never fully explained publicly. The firing came days after Fox News settled the Dominion Voting Systems defamation lawsuit for $787.5 million, a settlement that included the public release of internal communications showing Fox hosts, including Carlson, had privately dismissed the stolen election claims they were broadcasting to millions of viewers every night.[54]

Carlson was not a Trump loyalist in the personal sense. He was an ideological architect. Where Trump operated on instinct, Carlson operated on argument. He gave the movement's grievances intellectual form. He connected economic anxiety to cultural displacement to institutional corruption in a nightly monologue that millions of working-class Americans found more honest than anything else on television. When he was gone, that function disappeared with him.

Carlson launched an independent platform, built a massive audience that followed him out of the Fox ecosystem, and in 2024 campaigned actively for Trump's second term. He conducted the famous interview with Trump that aired simultaneously with the first Republican primary debate, which Trump skipped to sit with Carlson instead. By June 2025 he was publicly criticizing the administration's foreign policy. In April 2026, on his podcast, he apologized to his audience for having misled them into voting for Trump. He described it as a cause of personal torment.[55]

The man who had done more than perhaps anyone outside of Trump himself to build the intellectual and emotional case for the MAGA movement was now, to the same audience he had built, making the case that the movement had betrayed everything it had promised.

Charlie Kirk: The Machine That Organized the Young

Charlie Kirk was assassinated on September 10, 2025, at Utah Valley University in Orem, Utah. He was 31 years old. He had just finished answering a question about gun violence when a single shot fired from a campus rooftop struck him in the neck. A 22-year-old named Tyler Robinson was taken into custody after his own parents identified him from images released by the FBI. He is currently facing aggravated murder charges. Prosecutors are seeking the death penalty.[56]

Kirk had spent thirteen years building Turning Point USA from a kitchen table operation into an $85 million-a-year organization with a $64 million endowment and a demonstrable capacity to move young voters. He was also one of the most committed free speech absolutists in American conservative media. He had built his entire brand around the argument that speech, however offensive, however ugly, however wrong, is protected and must be protected.

Within days of his assassination, the movement built on that principle inverted it completely. Vice President JD Vance guest-hosted Kirk's own podcast on September 15, 2025, and urged listeners that when they saw someone celebrating Kirk's murder they should "call them out, and hell, call their employer." Attorney General Pam Bondi stated publicly that the government would "absolutely target you, go after you" for hate speech, a statement that directly contradicted decades of Supreme Court precedent that Kirk had spent his career defending. The organization Kirk founded to fight cancel culture became, in the weeks after his death, a justification for canceling people who said the wrong thing about him.[57]

The organizational collapse that followed was swift. His widow Erika Kirk was appointed CEO eight days after the assassination. Within months she had fired more than 60 staffers in what survivors called the TPUSA Purge. Seven university student governments rejected chapter applications. Candace Owens began promoting conspiracy theories that Kirk had been murdered by insiders. The University of Georgia chapter president, Caroline Mattox, resigned publicly in April 2026 after attending a TPUSA event featuring Vice President Vance, writing that the organization had "strayed so far from its original purpose and principles" and was spreading "blatant dishonesty."[58]

The organization that Kirk built to deliver young voters to the Republican coalition heading into 2026 cannot, as of this writing, win a utility board race in its own backyard in Arizona.

The Never-Trumpers Who Became the Last Men Standing

The people currently standing beside Donald Trump as his coalition fractures are in significant numbers the same people who declared him finished, morally bankrupt, and unfit for office in the days immediately following January 6, 2021. They returned when returning became advantageous. And the people who had defended Trump when defending him cost something real are the ones now being called traitors.

Mitch McConnell stood on the Senate floor on February 13, 2021, immediately after voting to acquit Trump on the second impeachment, and stated that Trump was "practically and morally responsible" for the events of January 6. McConnell subsequently endorsed Trump for the 2024 presidential election.[59]

Kevin McCarthy was recorded in a private call with Republican colleagues in the days after January 6 saying he was going to call Trump and tell him it was time to resign, that Trump's conduct was "atrocious and totally wrong." Those recordings were published by the New York Times in April 2022. McCarthy had publicly denied making those statements before the recordings surfaced. He subsequently became one of Trump's most reliable institutional allies.[60]

Lindsey Graham, standing in the Senate chamber in the early hours of January 7, 2021, stated: "All I can say is count me out. Enough is enough." He subsequently counted himself back in.

Liz Cheney, who voted to impeach Trump and served as vice chair of the January 6 Select Committee, has not reversed her position. She is considered a traitor by the movement. Mitt Romney retired from the Senate rather than run in an environment he described as incompatible with honest public service. Adam Kinzinger did not seek reelection. They are gone. The people who said what they said on January 7 and then walked it back are still there.

Opinion

This inversion is, in the author's view, the most clarifying fact about the current state of American conservatism. The MAGA movement was built on the premise that Washington was populated by people who said whatever was necessary to maintain their positions and then did the opposite once the cameras turned off. The people now running the institutional apparatus of that movement are, by their own documented words and subsequent actions, precisely those people. The base that built the movement to break that pattern has been handed a movement now run by its embodiment.

The coalition that swept into power in November 2024 had lost its ideological architect to a corporate firing, its youth organizing infrastructure to an assassination, and its philosophical consistency to the calculated self-preservation of the same institutional class it had spent a decade promising to dismantle. What remained was a movement that looked like MAGA from the outside and functioned like the establishment it had replaced.

Conclusion

The Swamp He Built

What the Record Shows

On June 16, 2015, a man descended a gold escalator and told the country that the people running it were corrupt, that the system was rigged, that the donors owned the politicians, that the wars were stupid, that the trade deals were destroying the working class, that the border was a disgrace, that the debt was a catastrophe, and that he alone was positioned to fix it because he alone had no one to answer to.

The wall was not built. Mexico did not pay. The national debt grew by $7 trillion in the first term and is on a trajectory to reach 120 percent of GDP within a decade. The swamp was not drained. It was restocked, first with industry insiders and donors in the first term, and then, in the second, with a structure so openly transactional that the president's personal financial instruments became the mechanism for purchasing access to the executive branch. Medicaid was cut by $800 billion over ten years by a man who had promised explicitly and repeatedly that he would never cut Medicaid. DOGE promised $2 trillion in savings and delivered, by independent verification, less than $2 billion while increasing the overall federal spending baseline.

The Pattern

The more disturbing possibility is that the system Trump promised to dismantle is not dismantlable by the methods he used, because the methods required to win the system are the same methods that make you the system once you have won. Every concession made to those requirements is a concession of the outsider identity that justified the campaign. By the time you have made enough concessions to actually govern, you have become what you ran against.

This is not a Trump-specific problem. It is a structural problem with American politics that Trump identified more clearly than almost anyone before him and then demonstrated more completely than anyone before him by failing to solve it in exactly the ways he said others had failed.

What Both Parties Owe the Record

The Republican Party owes the record an honest accounting of what it enabled. It enabled the January 6 attack by spending two months validating false claims of election fraud it knew to be false. It enabled the second term's fiscal recklessness by abandoning the deficit hawk principles it had claimed as its identity for forty years the moment a Republican was doing the spending. It enabled the corruption of the second term by confirming cabinet nominees whose conflicts of interest it would have treated as disqualifying under any Democratic president.

The Democratic Party owes the record an honest accounting of what it failed to do. It failed to offer a credible economic alternative to working-class voters who had legitimate grievances. It failed to address the southern border with the seriousness the situation required. It ran an incumbent president it knew was cognitively compromised and spent two years calling anyone who said so a disinformation agent, then confirmed the truth of what it had denied within weeks of a single debate. It replaced that president through an internal process with no democratic accountability and then asked voters to celebrate the result.

The Coalition

The people who voted for Donald Trump in 2024 were not wrong about the diagnosis. The system is corrupt. The donor class does purchase political outcomes. The trade deals did hurt working-class communities. The border was not enforced. The wars were stupid. The debt is a catastrophe. Washington does run for the benefit of the people inside it rather than the people outside it.

They were not wrong about the diagnosis. What the record examined in this investigation suggests is that the doctor they chose had the same disease he promised to cure, and that the cure he administered made the patient sicker.

Whether that conclusion is correct is for the reader to decide. The documented record on which it is based is not in dispute.

What Comes Next

This investigation ends in June 2026. The 2026 midterms have not yet been held. The full consequences of the One Big Beautiful Bill have not yet been felt by the households whose Medicaid coverage it will affect. The Trump-Carlson feud has not resolved. The TPUSA collapse has not stabilized. The Epstein wound has not closed. The national debt continues to compound. The approval rating continues to sit ten points below where it was on inauguration day.

What comes next is not the subject of this investigation. This investigation's subject was what happened, documented in sequence, from the escalator to here. The record is complete. The reader is capable of drawing their own conclusions from it.

That is, and has always been, the only point.
