The Tab We Cannot Pay

How Two Generations of Policy Left Millennials and Gen Z Holding the Bill

Prologue

What We Were Born Into

I was born on November 22nd, 2000. The last year of the old world, as it turns out, though none of us knew it at the time.

I was ten months old on September 11, 2001. I have no memory of it. I grew up in the world it created without ever knowing there had been a different one. The security lines, the body scanners, the wars on the television, the color-coded threat levels, all of it was just the way things were. Normal. I had no before to compare it to. None of us born around that time did. We were handed a country already transformed and told this was just how America worked.

I grew up believing the wars were justified. I had no reason not to. The teachers did not question it. The news did not question it, at least not in any way that reached me. The official story was that we were attacked, that we had to respond, that the people running the country knew things we did not and were making hard decisions to keep us safe. I accepted that. I was a kid. Most kids do. It was not until much later, after a completely different crisis forced me to start asking questions, that I went back and looked at what the record actually showed.

I was seven years old turning eight in 2008. I did not know what a financial crisis was. I did not know anyone who lost their house. The recession was something adults talked about on television and it did not land in my life in any way I could feel or name at the time. I have since read a great deal about what happened, about the mortgage-backed securities and the credit default swaps and the specific decisions made by specific people in specific institutions that produced that collapse, and about who got bailed out and who did not. But I want to be honest: I did not live through 2008 with any awareness of its injustice. I learned that injustice later, the same way I learned about most things in this piece, after COVID taught me that the official version of events was worth examining.

I was twelve turning thirteen in 2013. That was the year something shifted in the way information moved through American life, though I would not have been able to name it then or for years after. I was just a kid on the internet. I was not reading media criticism or studying platform algorithms. What I can say is that by the time I was in high school the political conversation around me had become louder and angrier in a way that felt different from before, and that the loudness seemed to generate more heat than light. I have written about this in detail elsewhere on this site, about how the media fracture of that era was not accidental but structural, built into the business model of the platforms and the outlets that came to dominate American information. What it did to my generation is something I only began to understand in retrospect.

Then came March 2020. I was nineteen years old and the government told me to lock myself in my house.

I want to be careful here, because the pandemic killed over a million Americans and that grief is real and belongs to real people. But I was nineteen. I was not in a high-risk category. And what was being asked of me, and of everyone my age, was to give up the years that do not come back, the social architecture of early adulthood, the relationships and the professional footing and the simple experience of being young and free, based on the instructions of institutions I had, until that point, largely trusted without examination.

And then those institutions started contradicting themselves. And then the story started changing. And then I started asking a question that I have not been able to stop asking since: if they were willing to be this careless with the truth about this, what else had they been careless about?

I started reading. I could not stop. The 2008 financial crisis. The Iraq War and the intelligence that was not there. The surveillance programs that had been running for years before Edward Snowden made them public. The ways in which the political conversation my generation had inherited had been deliberately shaped to keep us focused on each other rather than on the people making the decisions. The more I read, the more the pieces fit together in a way that nobody had ever laid out plainly for me.

And while all of that was happening, while my generation was losing the formative years we will never recover, something else was happening simultaneously that nobody was talking about loudly enough. The wealth of the world's ten richest men doubled. U.S. billionaire wealth grew by over a trillion dollars in the first year of the pandemic alone. The Federal Reserve pumped trillions into financial markets to stabilize the economy, and the vast majority of that money flowed to people who already owned assets, which is to say people who were not us. The largest transfer of wealth in modern recorded history happened during a public health emergency, while my generation was locked in its apartments watching it on screens.

This piece is not a complaint. Complaining is not a policy argument and it does not change anything. It is an attempt to lay out, in documented and sourced terms, what the arithmetic of the last forty years actually looks like for the generation that is now being asked to build a life inside the wreckage of decisions it had no part in making. The wages that did not keep up. The housing that priced us out. The debt we were told to take on because it would be worth it. The cost of starting a family that now looks more like a financial crisis than a life milestone. The retirement that the math suggests may not be there when we arrive.

I did not grow up angry. I grew up trusting. COVID ended that. What replaced it was not cynicism but curiosity, and a refusal to accept the official version of anything without looking at the actual record first.

This is what the record shows.

Part I

The Wage Illusion

Every generation is told that if you work hard, you will get ahead. My generation was told this too. The problem is that the arithmetic stopped working somewhere along the way, and nobody in a position of power has been particularly honest about when it happened or why.

Let us start with the basic numbers.

Nominal wages, meaning the raw dollar figure on your paycheck before any adjustment for what that dollar actually buys, have gone up. That is true. The Bureau of Labor Statistics documents that the nominal average weekly wage rose from approximately $683 in March 2006 to $1,283 by early 2026, an increase of roughly 88 percent on paper. Politicians on both sides of the aisle have pointed to numbers like this as evidence that the economy is working. What they leave out is the second half of the calculation. Once you adjust for inflation, that same wage grew from the equivalent of approximately $1,142 to $1,283 in real terms, a roughly 12 percent increase over twenty years.[1]

Twelve percent over twenty years. That is what the recovery actually looked like for the average American worker.

And that average obscures something important. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland documented in February 2026 that real wages in the bottom half of the wage distribution were relatively flat from 2001 through the mid-2010s. Not growing slowly. Flat.[2] The workers who needed wage growth the most, the ones with the least margin for error in their household budgets, were the ones who got the least of it.

The Pew Research Center published an analysis in May 2026 examining whether American wages had kept up with inflation across a twenty-six year period from 1999 to 2025. Their finding was that the answer depends heavily on which inflation measure you use and where in the wage distribution you are looking. For workers in the middle and lower ranges, the picture is considerably worse than the headline numbers suggest.[3]

The worst single period in recent memory was 2021 and 2022. Nominal wages were growing, which the administration at the time pointed to as evidence of a strong labor market. Inflation was growing faster. In June 2022, inflation hit 9.1 percent while nominal wage growth was running at 4.8 percent, a gap of 4.3 percentage points. In practical terms, workers who received raises that year lost ground anyway.[4]

Opinion

Here is what I think is worth saying plainly about this. The wage stagnation documented above did not happen because of impersonal economic forces that nobody could have anticipated or controlled. It happened alongside specific policy decisions: the decline of union density, which both parties presided over and neither seriously reversed; trade agreements that both parties supported and that restructured the labor market in ways that benefited capital over workers; a Federal Reserve mandate that historically treated rising wages as an inflation risk to be suppressed rather than an economic good to be protected. The economy did not accidentally stop working for workers. It was managed, by both parties, in ways that consistently prioritized other outcomes. The record on this is not ambiguous.

What this means for my generation specifically is the following. We entered the labor market either during or in the immediate aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis or during the COVID shutdown of 2020. Research has consistently documented that workers who enter the labor market during a recession experience wage penalties that persist for a decade or more. The starting point matters enormously because wages compound. If your first job pays less than it should, and your raises are calculated as percentages of that lower base, the gap between where you are and where you should be widens every year even when the economy is technically growing. My generation did not just get a bad hand in 2008 or 2020. We got a starting line that was set back, and we have been running from behind ever since.

The nominal wage numbers went up. The real purchasing power did not keep pace. And the people who designed the policies that produced that outcome have spent the intervening years arguing about everything except that.

Part II

The Housing Crisis

There is a number that explains more about the financial condition of my generation than almost any other single data point. In the 1990s, the median American home cost roughly 3.2 times the median annual household income. Today it costs five times that income, and in the years immediately following the pandemic it reached as high as 5.6 times, the highest ratio ever recorded in the United States dating back to the early 1970s when the data was first tracked systematically.[1]

That ratio is not an abstraction. It is the difference between a generation that could buy homes in their mid to late twenties and a generation that statistically cannot. The median age of the American first-time homebuyer was 29 in 1980. By 2025 it had risen to 40, according to the National Association of Realtors' 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers.[2]

Correction note: The NAR's 40-year median is the most widely cited figure and reflects NAR's annual survey of recent buyers. Other data sources give a lower figure: the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and the Mortgage Bankers Association both place the median age of first-time buyers closer to 32 to 33 as of 2024 to 2025, citing different methodology and data sources. The directional trend, that first-time buyers are considerably older than they were a generation ago, is not disputed across any of these sources. The precise age varies by how "first-time buyer" is defined and how the sample is drawn.

The raw numbers make the point without any editorializing. In 1985, the median American home cost $82,800. By 2025 that figure had reached $416,900, an increase of roughly 415 percent. Over the same period, median household income rose from $23,620 to $83,150, an increase of approximately 255 percent.[3] Home prices grew at nearly twice the rate of income over four decades. And that national average understates the situation in the markets where young people actually want to live and work. In Los Angeles the median home price is 12.5 times the median annual household income. In San Jose it is 10.5 times. In New York it is 9.8 times.[4]

Between 2019 and 2024, median single-family home prices rose by nearly 48 percent nationally. Median income rose by 22 percent over the same period.[5] By 2025 a first-time buyer needed a minimum annual income of approximately $126,700 to afford the median priced home in the United States.[6] The median household income that year was $83,150. The gap is $43,550 per year, and it represents a complete structural disconnection between the housing market and the population it is supposed to serve.

Between 2020 and 2021, mortgage rates hit historic lows. Millions of American homeowners bought or refinanced at rates below 4 percent, in many cases below 3 percent. As of the second quarter of 2025, more than half of all American homeowners with mortgages had interest rates below 4 percent, against a prevailing 30-year fixed rate that has been hovering above 6.5 percent for the better part of three years.[7]

The Federal Housing Finance Agency calculated in a primary research paper that this lock-in effect prevented 1.72 million home sales between 2022 and 2024, and that the resulting reduction in supply pushed home prices up an additional 7 percent above where they would otherwise have been.[8] Federal Reserve Board economists confirmed in a 2025 paper that the lock-in effect explains 44 percent of the total drop in mortgage borrower mobility between 2021 and 2022.[9]

To summarize: the people who already own homes have a powerful financial incentive not to sell them. The people who do not own homes cannot afford to buy them. The inventory that would normally move between those two groups has largely stopped moving. And the generation that is stuck on the outside of this frozen market is mine.

Opinion

This did not happen by accident. The political decisions that produced it span decades and implicate both parties equally. Exclusionary zoning laws, which restrict the construction of multi-family housing in vast swaths of American cities and suburbs, have been protected and expanded by local and state governments controlled at various times by both Democrats and Republicans, typically at the urging of existing homeowners who benefit financially from supply constraints. The mortgage interest deduction, a federal tax subsidy that primarily benefits people who already own expensive homes, has survived every serious budget negotiation for forty years with bipartisan protection. The financialization of the single-family housing market, meaning the entry of large institutional investors buying homes as investment vehicles rather than residences, accelerated under policy frameworks that neither party moved seriously to restrict. The housing crisis is not a natural disaster. It is the accumulated output of specific choices made by specific people who were, in most cases, already homeowners themselves.

Part III

The Rent Trap

If you cannot buy, you rent. My generation rents. We rent in numbers and at costs that have no precedent in the modern American housing record, and the data on what that is doing to household finances is not complicated or ambiguous. It is simply bad, and it is getting worse.

The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies' 2025 report documented that in 2023, a record 22.6 million renter households in the United States were cost-burdened, meaning they spent more than 30 percent of their income on rent and utilities. That figure represents exactly half of all renter households in the country.[1] Within that record number, 12.1 million households, representing 27 percent of all renters, were severely cost-burdened, meaning they spent more than half of their income on housing.[2]

The cost burden has risen in 43 of 50 states since before the pandemic.[3] Since 2001, the share of renters earning between $45,000 and $74,999 annually who are cost-burdened has doubled, reaching 45 percent by 2023. Even among renters earning $75,000 or more, 13 percent were cost-burdened in 2023.[4]

Nationally, rents are up 26 percent since early 2020.[5] There are 2.5 million fewer units available for less than $600 per month than there were in 2013. There are 5 million fewer units available for between $600 and $1,000 per month than a decade ago.[6] The median asking rent for apartments completed in the fourth quarter of 2024 was $1,900 per month, affordable only to households earning at least $76,000 annually.[7]

Now for the part of this story that does not get told loudly enough.

After the 2008 financial crisis produced a wave of foreclosures, private equity firms and institutional investors moved into the single-family housing market at a scale that had no precedent in American history. The U.S. Government Accountability Office documented in a 2024 report that before the crisis, no single firm owned more than 1,000 single-family homes. By 2022, 32 institutional investors collectively owned 450,000 single-family homes. The five largest alone owned nearly 300,000.[8]

Blackstone blazed the trail. In 2012 the firm formed Invitation Homes, deployed capital at a reported pace of over $100 million per week buying up properties, and built what became the largest single-family rental landlord in the United States, ultimately controlling over 80,000 homes concentrated in Florida, California, Atlanta, Phoenix, and Chicago.[9] The GAO documented that institutional investors now hold 25 percent of the single-family rental market in metro Atlanta, 21 percent in Jacksonville, 18 percent in Charlotte, and 15 percent in Tampa.[10]

In September 2024 the Federal Trade Commission took action against Invitation Homes for deceiving renters about lease costs, charging undisclosed junk fees, failing to inspect homes before residents moved in, and unfairly withholding security deposits. Invitation Homes agreed to a $48 million settlement.[11] In December 2025 the FTC secured a $23 million settlement against Greystar, the country's largest landlord overall, for similarly misleading consumers about monthly rent costs through hidden fees.[12]

Then there is RealPage. In August 2024 the Department of Justice, joined by eight states, filed an antitrust lawsuit against RealPage, alleging that it used nonpublic, competitively sensitive pricing information shared among participating landlords to reduce competition in local rental markets. RealPage reached a proposed settlement with the DOJ in November 2025.[13]

Correction note: The November 2025 proposed settlement did not include financial penalties or a legal finding that RealPage or participating landlords committed antitrust violations. RealPage denied wrongdoing. The settlement required RealPage to stop sharing certain categories of competitively sensitive data and submit to DOJ monitoring. The underlying allegations of market coordination have not been adjudicated.

The rent crisis is not simply a supply and demand problem. It is a market that was restructured, after 2008, by the entry of institutional capital that bought up the housing stock ordinary people were losing, converted it into a yield-generating asset class, and then in documented cases engaged in pricing practices that regulators found harmful to renters who had no alternative. My generation did not stumble into this market. We were born into it.

Opinion

The rent trap is the direct downstream consequence of the housing crisis described in Part II, and both of them flow from the same source: a political class on both sides that has for decades treated housing primarily as a wealth-building vehicle for people who already own it rather than as a basic necessity for people who do not. The Fannie Mae program that helped institutional investors acquire foreclosed properties at scale in 2012 was a government program. The policy frameworks that allowed the single-family rental industry to consolidate without serious regulatory intervention spanned two administrations of different parties. The RealPage antitrust case was filed under Biden and settled under Trump. Neither party built the wall that would have kept this from happening because neither party had a strong financial incentive to do so. The donor class and the political class overlap considerably when it comes to real estate.

Part IV

The Debt Factory

From the time we were old enough to understand what college was, we were told the same thing. Go to school, get a degree, and the debt will be worth it. It was the central financial promise made to my generation by parents, teachers, guidance counselors, and politicians of both parties across the entirety of our childhoods. What nobody told us was that the system making that promise had been quietly engineered, over decades, to extract maximum debt from the people it was supposedly serving.

In 1980, tuition and fees at a four-year public college ran approximately $11,840 per year in today's dollars. By the 2024 to 2025 academic year that figure had reached $24,920 for in-state students at public institutions, $30,780 for out-of-state students, and $43,350 at private nonprofit four-year colleges.[1] The Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index for college tuition and fees documents that $20,000 worth of college in 1980 would cost approximately $270,000 by 2025 under the same measure.[2]

General consumer prices roughly doubled between 1980 and 2025. College costs more than tripled.[3] The average annual rate of college tuition inflation has historically run well above the general inflation rate, estimated at roughly 6 to 8 percent annually in peak periods, against a general inflation rate that has averaged 3 to 4 percent over the same span.[4] From 2002 to 2022, general CPI inflation ran at approximately 39 percent. Tuition inflation over the same twenty-year period ran at 68 percent.[5]

Total student loan debt in the United States currently stands at $1.833 trillion. The outstanding federal student loan balance alone is $1.693 trillion, owed by 42.8 million borrowers.[6] The average federal student loan balance per borrower reached $39,633 as of December 2025, a record high. The total average balance including private loan debt is estimated at approximately $43,333 per borrower.[7] As of July 2025, 3.6 million borrowers owe more than $100,000 in student loan debt, an increase of 1.1 million borrowers from 2018.[8]

The federal student loan debt total was approximately $481 billion in early 2006. By 2012 it had doubled. It has more than tripled since then.[9]

The explosion in college costs did not happen in spite of federal student loan programs. It happened in significant part because of them. When the federal government guarantees loans to students regardless of the cost of the institution they are attending, it removes the primary market constraint that would otherwise limit how much colleges can charge. The colleges collect the tuition upfront. The federal government holds the loan. The student holds the risk.

In May 2025, the Trump administration restarted collections on defaulted loans, including wage garnishment, after the pandemic-era payment pause ended. As of early 2025, approximately 24 percent of borrowers with payments due were behind on those payments.[10]

Opinion

The student debt crisis is the most straightforward example in this piece of a problem that was predicted, documented, and ignored by the people in a position to address it. The relationship between federally guaranteed loans and tuition inflation has been studied and reported for decades. Both parties have used student debt as a political football, alternately promising relief and blocking it, without ever seriously addressing the underlying pricing structure that generates the debt in the first place. The Obama administration expanded income-based repayment options. The Biden administration attempted broad cancellation that the Supreme Court struck down. The Trump administration restarted collections and moved to dismantle repayment programs. None of these interventions touched the core mechanism: as long as colleges can raise tuition freely and the federal government guarantees the loans that cover the increase, the debt will keep growing. The people who designed this system went to college when it cost a fraction of what it costs today. They paid it off. They then spent their careers in the institutions that set the price, wrote the loans, and passed the legislation that kept the structure intact. The tab they handed us was not an accident. It was the output of a system working exactly as it was designed to work, for the people who designed it.

Part V

The Family Price Tag

There is a version of the American life that was sold to my generation as the goal. Finish school, get a job, find a partner, buy a house, start a family. The preceding sections of this piece have documented what happened to the first three steps of that sequence. This section is about the fourth. And the numbers are not encouraging.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture has been tracking the cost of raising a child in America since 1960. Adjusted for inflation to 2025 dollars, the cost of raising a child born today to age eighteen is estimated at approximately $320,000 to $322,000, depending on the inflation measure applied.[1]

Correction note: The USDA's Expenditures on Children by Families report, the primary government source for this data, was last published in 2017, covering children born in 2015. The $320,000 to $322,000 figure cited here is an extrapolation by secondary analysts applying recent inflation data to the USDA's methodology, not a current primary USDA publication. The direction of the trend is consistent with the cost trajectory the USDA documented through 2015, but readers should be aware this is an estimate, not a current primary government finding.

Some analyses using broader cost definitions put the figure as high as $414,000 for a middle-income family when current conditions are fully accounted for.[2] That figure does not include college. It covers birth to eighteen only.

The average annual cost of raising a child under five in the United States reached $27,743 in 2025, rising 4.5 percent between 2024 and 2025, outpacing the general inflation rate of 2.82 percent over the same period.[4] A 2025 Cost of Care Survey conducted by Care.com found that parents spent on average 22 percent of their household income on childcare in 2024. Average costs ranged from $343 per week for one child at a daycare center to $827 per week for one child receiving care from a nanny.[5]

To put the childcare number in direct context: the federal minimum wage is $7.25 per hour, unchanged since 2009. A full-time minimum wage worker earns approximately $15,080 per year before taxes. The average cost of putting one child in daycare, at $343 per week, is approximately $17,836 per year. A minimum wage worker cannot afford daycare for a single child on their entire annual income. At the median household income of $83,150, spending $17,836 on daycare represents 21 percent of gross household income, before rent, before student loan payments, before food, before healthcare.

Pew Research Center analysis published in 2025 found that the total number of children that men and women between the ages of 20 and 39 plan to have dropped from 2.3 in 2012 to 1.8 in 2023, falling below the replacement rate of 2.1 children per woman needed to maintain a stable population.[6] The U.S. fertility rate currently stands at 1.7 births per woman, a historic low.[7] CDC data published in April 2026 documents that birth rates among mothers under 30 decreased between 2024 and 2025, while birth rates among mothers over 30 increased.[8]

A CBS News survey found that nearly one in four Millennials and Gen Z adults say they will not have children due to finances.[9] A professor of population and family health at the Columbia School of Public Health summarized the condition of my generation plainly in a 2025 interview: rising costs including student debt, housing, and childcare make financial stability an uphill battle, and Gen Z does not view marriage and children as inevitable milestones because they have grown up in a world shaped by economic instability.[11]

That is not a cultural shift. That is a rational response to a financial reality. The people who built that reality are the same people who are now expressing concern about the birth rate.

Opinion

The United States is one of the only wealthy developed nations without a universal childcare system, without guaranteed paid parental leave, and without the kind of family support infrastructure that peer countries treat as a baseline rather than a luxury. The political conversation about the birth rate decline has focused almost entirely on cultural explanations, incentive programs, and rhetorical appeals to family values from politicians who have consistently voted against the policies that would make family formation financially viable for the generation they are appealing to. You cannot tell a generation that is carrying $40,000 in student debt, paying 40 percent of their income in rent, and facing $18,000 per year in childcare costs that the solution to the birth rate problem is a tax credit and a speech. The math does not respond to speeches. It responds to policy. And the policy record on this, across both parties, is one of prolonged and bipartisan failure to treat the cost of raising a family as a problem worth actually solving.

Part VI

The Healthcare Anchor

At age 26, American law requires you to come off your parents' health insurance. For my generation, that birthday marks the moment you enter a market that will consume a growing share of every paycheck you earn for the rest of your working life, in exchange for coverage that still leaves you financially exposed when you actually need to use it.

KFF's 2025 survey documented that the average annual premium for employer-sponsored family health coverage reached $26,993 in 2025. The worker's share of that premium averaged $6,850 for family coverage and $1,886 for single coverage.[1] Family health insurance now costs more per year than many Americans pay in rent. It costs more than a semester of tuition at a public university.

The average deductible for a worker with single coverage in 2024 was $1,787, 47 percent higher than it was a decade ago.[2] By 2025, 72 percent of covered workers faced an out-of-pocket maximum of more than $3,000 for single coverage, with one in five facing an out-of-pocket maximum above $6,000.[3] Workers at small firms face average deductibles of $2,631, nearly $1,000 more than workers at large firms.[4]

A family with a $6,000 out-of-pocket maximum, paying $6,850 in annual premium contributions, faces a potential healthcare expenditure of nearly $13,000 in a year when someone gets seriously ill, before a single other household expense is paid.

KFF data documents that out-of-pocket spending per person in the United States was $115 in 1970, equivalent to $703 in inflation-adjusted dollars. By 2023 that figure had reached $1,514 per person per year, more than double the inflation-adjusted 1970 baseline.[5] Family premiums increased 6 percent in the most recent survey year against wage increases of 4 percent and general inflation of 2.7 percent.[6]

In 2026, the average ACA Marketplace deductible grew by 37 percent in a single year, from $2,759 in 2025 to $3,786 in 2026, the steepest single-year increase on record, driven in significant part by the expiration of enhanced premium tax credits.[7] About 4 in 10 insured adults worry about affording their monthly health insurance premium. Sixty-two percent worry about affording their deductible before insurance coverage actually begins.[8]

Opinion

The United States spends more on healthcare per capita than any other wealthy nation on earth and produces worse outcomes on most major health metrics than the peer countries it is being compared against. During the drafting of the Affordable Care Act in 2009 and 2010, the healthcare industry spent more than $270 million on lobbying in 2009 alone, averaging $1.2 million per day for every day Congress was in session during the first three months of that year. The pharmaceutical companies and hospitals that backed the final bill did so after successfully lobbying to exclude a public option and drug price negotiations from the legislation. The key drafter of the Senate Finance Committee version of the bill, Elizabeth Fowler, came directly from her position as Vice President for Public Policy at WellPoint, the nation's largest health insurer at the time.[9]

It is worth noting that the public option's removal was not driven solely by lobbying. The Senate's 60-vote threshold in 2009 to 2010 meant the legislation needed every Democratic vote, and moderate senators including Joe Lieberman and Ben Nelson explicitly opposed the public option on policy grounds. The lobbying was real and well-documented; so were the legislative constraints. Both were factors. The Affordable Care Act expanded coverage for millions of Americans and represented a genuine policy achievement on access. It did not address the underlying cost structure, because addressing the underlying cost structure would have required overruling the industries that had spent over a billion dollars shaping the outcome and assembling a legislative coalition that did not exist in the Senate at the time.[10]

The Republican effort to repeal the ACA without replacement was not a cost solution either. The CBO estimated that the Senate's Better Care Reconciliation Act of 2017 would have resulted in 22 million more uninsured Americans by 2026. The repeal-without-replacement bill that came to a Senate vote in July 2017 was projected by CBO to leave 32 million Americans without coverage by 2026 and cause the individual insurance market to virtually collapse in 75 percent of the country.[11] My generation will spend more of its income on healthcare than any preceding generation, in a system that both parties have had decades to fix and neither has.

Part VII

The Retirement Mirage

There is a version of old age that the American economy used to promise its workers. You put in your years. Your employer contributed to a pension. When you retired, a check arrived every month for the rest of your life, guaranteed, regardless of what the stock market did. That promise, for most American workers, no longer exists.

Section 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code was enacted in 1978. It was not designed as a primary retirement savings vehicle. It was a tax provision allowing employees to defer compensation. The financial services industry recognized its potential as a replacement for employer-funded pensions and built an entire product ecosystem around it. By the time Gen X entered the workforce, the 401(k) had become the dominant model. By the time my generation entered the workforce, pensions outside of government employment had become a rarity.[1] The investment risk, the market risk, and the longevity risk all transferred from the employer to the worker. The Senate Special Committee on Aging received testimony in 2010 documenting this shift and noting that it significantly increased the risk that retirees would outlive their retirement income.[2]

Americans on average believe they will need approximately $1.46 million in savings to retire comfortably. At a standard 4 percent annual withdrawal rate, that amount would provide approximately $58,000 per year in retirement income, below the current median household income. To reach that target by retirement age, the average Millennial would need to contribute between $500 and $1,000 per month to a retirement account, starting now and continuing without interruption.[3]

The average Millennial has saved approximately $62,600 toward retirement.[4] A generation carrying average student debt of $39,000, paying above 30 percent of income in rent, facing childcare costs that consume a fifth of household income, and absorbing healthcare premiums that grow faster than wages every year is not a generation with $500 to $1,000 per month available for retirement contributions on top of everything else.

The Social Security Board of Trustees 2025 Annual Report projected that the combined Social Security trust fund will be depleted in 2034. After depletion, the program's tax income would be sufficient to pay approximately 81 percent of scheduled benefits.[5] The Congressional Budget Office, in its January 2025 baseline projections, projected the OASI trust fund specifically would be exhausted during 2033.[6] In March 2026, CBO testimony to the Senate Budget Committee projected exhaustion of the OASI fund as early as 2032.[7]

The situation deteriorated further when the One Big Beautiful Bill Act was signed into law on July 4, 2025. Social Security's chief actuary reported in August 2025 that the legislation would advance OASI trust fund depletion to late 2032, accelerating the insolvency timeline. On a combined OASDI basis, the projected depletion moved from the third quarter of 2034 to the first quarter of 2034.[8]

The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget documented in June 2025 that Social Security faces a 75-year actuarial deficit of approximately $26 trillion on a present value basis, the largest such shortfall since 1977. Restoring solvency would require the equivalent of at least a 22 percent reduction in benefits, a 29 percent increase in payroll taxes, or some combination of the two.[9]

My generation will reach full Social Security retirement age sometime around 2067. The trust fund is headed toward insolvency before most of us turn 40. The last structural intervention Congress made was the 1983 Social Security amendments, which raised the full retirement age from 65 to 67, representing an effective 13 percent benefit cut for workers born in 1960 or later. That change, made forty years ago, is the last serious structural intervention Congress has made.[10]

Opinion

The retirement crisis facing my generation is the most long-range of the documented failures in this piece, which is precisely why it has been the easiest for the political class to ignore. The consequences will not arrive on any current officeholder's watch. The people who made the decisions that produced this outcome will not be accountable when the bill comes due. My generation will be. We will be the ones explaining to our own children why the retirement that was promised to us was not there, using the same language that was used to explain every other broken promise documented in this piece: that it was complicated, that there were competing priorities, that nobody could have predicted it. The record shows that everyone predicted it. Nobody with the power to fix it chose to.

Part VIII

Who Did This

The preceding seven sections of this piece have documented, with primary sources, what the financial conditions of my generation actually look like. This section documents who built those conditions and how. No party escapes this section. The record does not permit it.

The pattern across forty years is consistent enough to have a shape. A problem develops. Studies document it. Hearings are held. Campaign promises are made. Legislation is passed that addresses the edges of the problem without touching its structural cause, usually because the structural cause is connected to a donor interest that neither party is willing to confront directly. The problem continues. A new administration takes office, from the other party, and the cycle repeats.

Wages

In August 1981, President Reagan issued a public ultimatum giving striking air traffic controllers 48 hours to return to work. On August 5, 1981, 11,345 controllers who remained on strike were terminated and banned from federal employment for life. The Federal Labor Relations Authority decertified PATCO on October 22, 1981. The signal sent to the private sector about the federal government's posture toward collective bargaining reverberated for decades. Union membership in the private sector has declined from approximately 20 percent in 1980 to under 6 percent today.[1]

The Clinton administration passed NAFTA in 1994 over significant labor opposition. The Economic Policy Institute documented that NAFTA contributed to the displacement of approximately 700,000 American manufacturing jobs, though it should be noted that EPI is a labor-aligned think tank and economists at other institutions have offered more mixed assessments of NAFTA's net employment effects. The manufacturing job losses associated with the period are real and documented; their precise attribution to NAFTA versus other forces including automation remains contested in the economics literature.[2] The same administration passed the China trade normalization legislation in 2000, accelerating manufacturing job losses that research estimates at between 2 and 2.4 million jobs between 1999 and 2011.[3]

The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 per hour since 2009, making the period from 2009 to the present the longest stretch without a federal minimum wage increase in the history of the federal minimum wage, established in 1938. The Obama administration supported minimum wage increases but did not achieve federal legislation. The Trump administration's first term did not pursue federal minimum wage increases. The Biden administration's proposal to raise the federal minimum wage to $15 per hour passed the House but died in the Senate.[4]

Housing

The mortgage interest deduction has survived every major tax reform effort since its modern form was established. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 under Reagan preserved it. Clinton-era budget negotiations preserved it. The Bush tax cuts of 2001 and 2003 preserved it. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 under Trump capped it but did not eliminate it. The Biden administration did not move to restructure it. At every point where the deduction could have been reformed, it was preserved by the bipartisan political will of the homeowning donor class.[5]

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999, which repealed the Glass-Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking, passed the Senate 90 to 8 and the House 362 to 57. It was signed by President Clinton. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000, which exempted credit default swaps from regulatory oversight, passed both chambers with broad bipartisan support and was signed by Clinton days before he left office.[6]

The post-crisis policy response that allowed institutional investors to purchase foreclosed homes at scale included a Fannie Mae program launched in 2012 under the Obama administration that allowed pre-qualified investors to bid on large portfolios of foreclosed properties. In 2017, Fannie Mae backed a $1 billion loan to Invitation Homes, the Blackstone-owned institutional landlord that had become the largest single-family rental company in the country.[7]

Student Debt

The Higher Education Act of 1965, signed by President Johnson, established the federal student loan program. It did not contain any mechanism to prevent colleges from raising tuition to capture the expanded loan availability.[8] The Middle Income Student Assistance Act of 1978, the Higher Education Amendments of 1992, the Student Loan Reform Act of 1993, the College Cost Reduction and Access Act of 2007, and the Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010 each expanded the program further. At no point in any of this legislative history did either party enact a mechanism that would have constrained tuition growth in exchange for access to federal loan dollars.[9]

The Biden administration announced broad debt cancellation in August 2022. The Supreme Court struck it down in Biden v. Nebraska in June 2023. The Trump administration restarted collections on defaulted loans in May 2025, including wage garnishment, while approximately 24 percent of borrowers with payments due remained behind on those payments.[10]

Healthcare

The ACA drafting process in 2009 involved over $1.1 billion in healthcare industry lobbying that, alongside legislative constraints including the Senate's 60-vote threshold and the opposition of moderate Democrats, resulted in exclusion of the public option and drug price negotiation. The Republican repeal effort in 2017 was projected by the CBO to leave between 22 and 32 million additional Americans uninsured depending on the version considered. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 authorized Medicare drug price negotiation for the first time, a genuine policy achievement, for a limited set of drugs. Neither party has produced a comprehensive legislative response to the cost structure that produces the premium and deductible numbers documented in Part VI.[11]

Retirement

The shift from defined benefit pensions to defined contribution 401(k) plans occurred under the watch of every administration from Reagan forward without a single significant federal legislative effort to reverse or counterbalance it. The Social Security solvency problem has been documented in the Trustees Annual Report every year since 1983. The last structural intervention was the 1983 amendments under Reagan. Every administration since has acknowledged the problem, proposed solutions, and failed to enact them. The current projected depletion date of 2032 to 2034, now accelerated by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed in July 2025, represents the accumulated cost of forty years of acknowledged inaction.[12]

The pattern is the same across every issue documented in this piece. Both parties have identified the problems. Both parties have promised solutions. Both parties have consistently prioritized the financial interests of the donor class over the policy interventions that would have produced different outcomes for the generation now living inside the results.

Part IX

What They Said vs. What They Did

Politicians have been promising to fix the conditions documented in this piece for as long as those conditions have existed. This section places the promises next to the record and lets the distance between them speak for itself.

On Wages and Workers

Ronald Reagan campaigned in 1980 on restoring the American worker's purchasing power and economic dignity. His administration's first major labor action was the termination of 11,345 striking air traffic controllers and the decertification of their union.[1]

Bill Clinton campaigned in 1992 on putting people first and explicitly promised to fight for American workers against trade deals that exported jobs. His administration passed NAFTA in 1993 and the China trade normalization bill in 2000, the two pieces of legislation that the Economic Policy Institute documents as responsible for the displacement of millions of manufacturing jobs, with the caveat that the net trade-off of those agreements is a contested question in economics, as noted in Part VIII.[2]

George W. Bush's administration allowed the federal minimum wage to remain at $5.15 per hour for the first six years of his presidency, the longest it had gone without an increase at that point in its history. It was raised under Democratic congressional pressure, not administration initiative.[3]

Barack Obama campaigned in 2008 on raising the minimum wage and strengthening union rights through the Employee Free Choice Act. The Employee Free Choice Act, which passed the House in 2007, was never brought to a Senate floor vote despite Democrats holding a Senate majority for two years of Obama's first term. The federal minimum wage was not raised during the Obama administration.[4]

Joe Biden campaigned in 2020 on a $15 federal minimum wage. The Raise the Wage Act passed the House in February 2021 and died in the Senate. The federal minimum wage remained at $7.25 per hour when Biden left office, exactly where it was when he took office and where it has been since 2009.[5]

On Housing

George W. Bush announced the American Dream Downpayment Initiative in 2003, pledging to help 5.5 million minority families become homeowners by the end of the decade. The underlying affordability gap widened throughout his administration. The deregulatory environment his administration maintained in the mortgage market contributed directly to the 2008 crisis that wiped out the homeownership gains of the preceding decade for millions of families.[6]

Barack Obama's administration launched the Home Affordable Modification Program, designed to help up to 4 million homeowners modify their loans. The Special Inspector General for TARP documented in multiple reports that HAMP helped approximately 887,000 homeowners permanently modify their loans while millions more lost their homes.[7] The administration simultaneously launched the Fannie Mae REO-to-Rental program that helped institutional investors acquire foreclosed properties at scale.

Joe Biden's 2020 campaign promised a $15,000 first-time homebuyer tax credit. The first-time homebuyer tax credit was not enacted during his administration. The housing price-to-income ratio reached record highs during his term.[8]

On Student Debt

Total student loan debt more than doubled during the Obama administration, from approximately $600 billion in 2009 to over $1.4 trillion in 2017, driven by rising tuition costs that his administration's policies did not structurally address.[9]

Joe Biden told voters during the 2020 campaign that he would cancel $10,000 in student debt immediately upon taking office. The cancellation did not happen immediately upon taking office. It was announced in August 2022, challenged in court, and struck down by the Supreme Court in June 2023. Total student loan debt continued rising throughout the Biden administration.[10]

Donald Trump's second administration moved to dismantle income-driven repayment programs, restarted collections on defaulted loans including wage garnishment in May 2025, and has not proposed structural legislation to address tuition inflation.[11]

On Healthcare

Bill Clinton campaigned in 1992 on universal healthcare coverage. The Clinton Health Security Act was introduced in 1993, assigned to five separate congressional committees, and never brought to a floor vote in either chamber despite Democrats controlling both houses of Congress for the first two years of his administration.[12]

George W. Bush signed the Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003, creating Medicare Part D. The legislation explicitly prohibited Medicare from negotiating drug prices directly with pharmaceutical companies, a provision inserted under industry lobbying pressure that locked in above-market drug costs for nearly two decades.[13]

Barack Obama campaigned on a public option as a centerpiece of his healthcare reform proposal. The public option was removed from the Affordable Care Act during the Senate drafting process, a product of both industry lobbying and the legislative reality that moderate Democratic senators including Joe Lieberman and Ben Nelson would not support it, making the 60-vote threshold unachievable with the public option included.[14]

Donald Trump campaigned in 2016 on repealing and replacing the ACA. His administration's replacement proposals were projected by the CBO to leave between 22 and 32 million more Americans uninsured and contained no mechanism for reducing underlying healthcare costs. The repeal effort failed in the Senate in July 2017.[15]

Joe Biden signed the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which authorized Medicare drug price negotiation for the first time, a genuine policy achievement. It was also the first time the prohibition enacted in 2003 under the Bush administration had been meaningfully challenged in twenty years.[16]

The Pattern

The promises documented above were made to real people by real politicians who, in many cases, believed them when they made them. This section does not argue universal bad faith. It argues something the record supports more precisely: that on each of these issues, when the moment came to choose between the financial interests of the donor class and the policy intervention that would have produced different outcomes for ordinary Americans, the choice was consistently made in favor of the donor class, across both parties, across four decades, regardless of which party held the White House or the Congress.

The voter was told the problem would be fixed. The donor was told the structure would be preserved. The donor's version of events is the one documented in the legislative record.

Opinion

I want to be precise about what I am and am not arguing here. I am not arguing that every politician is corrupt, that every promise is a lie, or that both parties are identical in every respect. They are not identical. There are genuine differences between the parties on social policy, on the scope of government, and on a range of issues that matter to real people. What I am arguing is that on the specific financial conditions documented in this piece, the conditions that determine whether my generation can afford to rent an apartment, service a student loan, raise a child, access healthcare, and retire with dignity, the two-party system has delivered forty years of acknowledged failure with bipartisan consistency. The anger that failure deserves has been redirected, with considerable skill and considerable investment, toward cultural and social conflicts that generate more political energy and less policy accountability. My generation inherited a country whose financial architecture was built by the people who also built the information environment designed to prevent us from noticing. COVID cracked that environment open for me. What I found on the other side of it is what this piece documents. The record is public. The arithmetic is not in dispute. What happens next is the only part that has not been written yet.

Conclusion

The Arithmetic Does Not Lie

Opinion — Everything in this conclusion is the author's analysis and perspective

My generation did not grow up angry. We grew up trusting. We were handed a set of institutions and told they worked, that the people running them were, on balance, trying to do right by the country, and that if we did what we were supposed to do, the life on the other side of it would be there waiting. Go to school. Take out the loans. Work hard. The system will meet you halfway.

The system did not meet us halfway.

The wages did not keep pace. The house was not there when we arrived at the down payment. The rent consumed the savings we were supposed to be building. The student debt compounded faster than the entry-level salary could service it. The child we were told would complete the picture cost more per year than a college education. The health insurance deductible wiped out the emergency fund we had been told to maintain. And the retirement account that was supposed to absorb all of this sits at a fraction of what the math requires, backed by a Social Security program that the Congressional Budget Office projects will be insolvent before most of us turn 40.

This is not a list of grievances. It is the output of a documented arithmetic. Every number in this piece came from a government agency, a congressional report, a primary research institution, or a court record. The record does not require interpretation. It requires only that someone read it.

Here is what else the record shows, and this is the part that does not get said loudly enough.

The people who built this system did not build it from the outside. They built it from the inside, from the offices and chambers and committee rooms where the laws were written and the votes were cast. And while they were writing those laws and casting those votes, they were also getting extraordinarily wealthy. The median net worth of a member of Congress is approximately $1 million. As of 2020, over half of the members of Congress were millionaires, in a country where approximately 5 to 12 percent of the general population holds that status. The median American household net worth, per Federal Reserve data, is approximately $97,300. The people legislating the conditions documented in this piece live in a financial universe that is categorically different from the one their constituents occupy.[1]

And they have been getting richer while in office. Ballotpedia's analysis of congressional wealth from 2004 to 2012 found that the median American citizen saw their household net worth decrease at an annual rate of 0.94 percent over that period, while members of Congress experienced a median annual increase of 1.55 percent. The average wealth of congressional representatives grew over the 2004 to 2014 period in real terms at a rate nearly seven times that of the 95th percentile of American households.[2]

The mechanism by which some of that enrichment occurs is not hidden. Members of Congress trade stocks in companies directly affected by the legislation they write and the committee hearings they attend. In 2008, members of Congress made stock trades in the days following a closed-door briefing on the Lehman Brothers collapse, given by the Secretary of the Treasury and the chair of the Federal Reserve on September 18, 2008, three days after Lehman failed. That activity was exposed in a 2011 CBS 60 Minutes investigation that produced national outrage. Congress responded by passing the Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act in 2012, which required disclosure of trades within 30 to 45 days. The penalty for violating it is $200.[3]

Two hundred dollars. For a member of Congress earning $174,000 in base salary, sitting on committees that move markets, trading stocks in the industries those committees regulate, the penalty for failing to disclose is two hundred dollars. The Campaign Legal Center documented in 2026 that during the 2025 government shutdown, while constituents navigated missed paychecks and drained benefits, lawmakers engaged in nearly 200 trades representing between $3 and $9 million in financial assets.[4]

The specific cases are documented and public. Senator Richard Burr sold between $628,000 and $1.72 million in stocks in February 2020, after the Senate Intelligence Committee, which he chaired, began receiving daily coronavirus briefings. The Department of Justice investigated; no charges were brought and the investigation was closed. Attorney General Pam Bondi sold Trump Media stock just before the tariff announcement in April 2025. Former Speaker Nancy Pelosi's husband has repeatedly timed trades around key policy announcements including electric vehicle infrastructure funding and microchip legislation. These are disclosed transactions in the public record, reviewed against the timing of public policy announcements.[5]

As of November 2025, at least 25 bills or resolutions had been introduced in the 119th Congress to strengthen or replace the STOCK Act. The Ban Congressional Stock Trading Act, S. 1879, was introduced in the 119th Congress. Neither had been enacted into law.[6] The bipartisan support for banning congressional stock trading, which polls at 86 percent approval across party lines, has produced the same outcome as the bipartisan support for addressing wages, housing, student debt, healthcare, and retirement documented across this piece: acknowledgment, hearings, press releases, and no structural change.[7]

Now these same people are campaigning on giving my generation a raise.

They stand at podiums and tell us the system is rigged against working people. They post about housing affordability and student debt relief and the cost of childcare. They say the right things in the right rooms in front of the right cameras. And then they go back to their offices and trade stocks in the pharmaceutical companies whose drug prices they just held a hearing about, and the defense contractors whose budgets they just approved, and the real estate investment trusts that own the apartment buildings my generation cannot afford to leave.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not saying the system cannot be changed. I am not saying the people inside it are uniformly corrupt or that every vote is bought. I am not saying my generation should burn anything down. What I am saying, and what the record supports, is that the change that is needed will not come from waiting for the people who benefit from the current structure to voluntarily dismantle it. It will come from the generation that inherited the wreckage deciding, in sufficient numbers, to use the legal and democratic tools available to demand something different.

That means voting in every election at every level, because the zoning laws that prevent housing construction are made at the city council level, and the state legislators who set tuition policy are elected in off-year races that most people ignore. It means demanding specific, structural commitments from candidates rather than accepting the ambient language of concern that produces no legislation. It means supporting the organizations doing the investigative and accountability work that makes the documented record in this piece possible, including ProPublica, the Brennan Center for Justice, The Intercept, and Dropsite News.[8] It means passing a law that actually bans congressional stock trading with penalties proportionate to the offense, and it means electing people who will pass it rather than people who will hold a hearing about it and then go back to trading.

The arithmetic of what my generation has inherited is not in dispute. The record of how it was built is public. The tools to change it are legal and democratic and available.

COVID opened my eyes. I started reading and I could not stop. What I found was not a conspiracy. It was something more straightforward and in some ways more troubling than a conspiracy. It was a system working exactly as designed, for the people who designed it, documented in plain sight in congressional records and court filings and financial disclosures that anyone can read.

You are reading one of them now.
