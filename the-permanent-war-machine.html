The Permanent War Machine

Who Profits, Who Pays, and Why America Cannot Stop Fighting — A documented examination of the political, financial, and media architecture of perpetual American war

Introduction

The Loop

On the morning of September 8, 2002, the New York Times published a front-page story reporting that Iraq had been seeking to purchase aluminum tubes for use in uranium enrichment. That same morning, Vice President Dick Cheney appeared on NBC's Meet the Press and cited the Times story as independent confirmation of the intelligence. What Cheney did not mention was that the Times story was itself sourced, at least in part, to information that had originated from his own office. He was citing himself, laundered through a newspaper, presented to the American public as independent corroboration.[1]

This loop — the government plants a story, the media reports it uncritically, the government then cites the media report as proof — is not an accident or an anomaly. It is a method. It has been used to sell every major American military intervention of the past half century. It depends on a press corps that is either too credulous, too embedded in the establishment, or too financially entangled with the very industries that profit from war to ask the obvious questions. And it works, because by the time the lie is exposed, the bombs have already fallen, the contracts have already been awarded, and the next crisis is already being manufactured.

This investigation describes the machine that produces these lies: who built it, who funds it, who operates it, who profits from it, and who pays the price. The evidence is not circumstantial. It is documented in congressional records, declassified intelligence assessments, SEC filings, Pentagon contracts, and the admissions of the participants themselves. The machine is not hidden. It operates in plain sight, sustained by the shared interests of politicians, contractors, lobbyists, think tanks, and media organizations that have made permanent war the default condition of American life.

And underpinning it all is one of the most important and least discussed facts in American political life: that when it comes to war, there is no real left and no real right in Washington. There is only the party of the military-industrial complex, and a small number of honest dissenters on both ends of the spectrum who have been saying so for decades and have been systematically ignored, mocked, and marginalized for their trouble.

Part One

The Blueprint

The Project for the New American Century

To understand the post-9/11 wars, you have to understand where they were conceived. Not in the rubble of the World Trade Center in September 2001, but in a Washington think tank four years earlier. In June 1997, a group of neoconservative intellectuals and policy figures founded the Project for the New American Century, known as PNAC. Its statement of principles called for a significant increase in defense spending, the challenging of regimes hostile to American values, and the promotion of political and economic freedom abroad by force if necessary. Among its founding signatories were Dick Cheney, Donald Rumsfeld, Paul Wolfowitz, Jeb Bush, and Elliott Abrams.[2]

In January 1998, PNAC sent a letter to President Clinton explicitly calling for the military removal of Saddam Hussein from power in Iraq. The signatories included Rumsfeld, Wolfowitz, Richard Perle, Douglas Feith, and David Wurmser. All five would hold senior positions in the administration that launched the Iraq War three years later.[3]

"A New Pearl Harbor"

In September 2000, PNAC published a 90-page strategic blueprint titled "Rebuilding America's Defenses." The document called for a massive expansion of American military power, the establishment of permanent bases in the Middle East, and the transformation of the U.S. military into a force capable of fighting multiple simultaneous wars. It acknowledged the transformation would be slow — then added a sentence that has haunted the document ever since: the process was likely to be slow "absent some catastrophic and catalyzing event, like a new Pearl Harbor."[4]

PNAC — "Rebuilding America's Defenses," September 2000, p. 51

"[T]he process of transformation, even if it brings revolutionary change, is likely to be long absent some catastrophic and catalyzing event — like a new Pearl Harbor."

Thirteen months after that document was published, the catastrophic and catalyzing event arrived. The individuals who had spent years writing that exactly such an event was needed were the same individuals who, within days, were directing the American military response. The agenda PNAC had been unable to move through ordinary democratic persuasion was enacted wholesale in the weeks and months that followed. The through-line is not inference. It is a paper trail: the blueprint written in 2000, the personnel in place in 2001, the policies enacted in 2002 and 2003.[5]

The Bureaucratic Reason

In May 2003, with the invasion of Iraq complete and no weapons of mass destruction found, Paul Wolfowitz sat down for an interview with Vanity Fair. He was asked why the administration had chosen weapons of mass destruction as the primary public justification for the war. His answer was remarkable. "For bureaucratic reasons," he said, "we settled on one issue, weapons of mass destruction, because it was the one reason everyone could agree on."[6]

The Pentagon subsequently released its own transcript, and some have argued Wolfowitz's full answer was more nuanced than the Vanity Fair framing suggested. That is a fair technical observation. But it is difficult, in light of everything that followed — no weapons found, the Senate Intelligence Committee's conclusion that assessments were not supported by the underlying intelligence, the Downing Street Memo's contemporaneous record that the facts were being fixed around a policy already decided — to read Wolfowitz's words as anything other than a confirmation that the case for war was constructed around a conclusion that had been reached before the evidence was assembled.

Part Two

The Profit Motive

The Warning That Was Ignored

On January 17, 1961, President Dwight D. Eisenhower delivered his farewell address. He was a five-star general, Supreme Commander of Allied Forces in Europe during the Second World War, and a two-term Republican president. He was not a radical. He was not a pacifist. And in his final words to the country he had served for fifty years, he issued a warning that has gone almost entirely unheeded. "In the councils of government," he said, "we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex. The potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist."[7]

The Numbers

The scale of what that system has become would have staggered even Eisenhower. In 2000, Lockheed Martin had revenues of approximately $25 billion. By 2022, that figure had grown to over $65 billion, almost entirely from government contracts. Raytheon, General Dynamics, Boeing Defense, and Northrop Grumman all saw revenues roughly double or triple in the two decades following September 11 — the two decades of continuous American war.[8]

$65B

Lockheed Martin revenues in 2022, up from $25 billion in 2000 — a 160% increase driven almost entirely by two decades of continuous American war.

The privatization of war accelerated this dynamic further. Private military contractors like Blackwater, SAIC, and Halliburton's KBR subsidiary received hundreds of billions in contracts to perform functions previously done by the military itself. A Congressional commission established specifically to examine wartime contracting estimated that between $31 billion and $60 billion of American taxpayer money was lost to waste and fraud in those contracts alone.[9][10]

The incentive is not subtle. When the United States is at war, these companies make money. When it is at peace, they do not. The financial interest of the largest sector of the American defense economy is structurally aligned with perpetual conflict.

Part Three

The Revolving Door

How the Machine Sustains Itself

The mechanism by which defense industry money translates into government policy is not primarily campaign donations, though those matter. It is the revolving door: the continuous movement of individuals between senior positions in the Pentagon, Congress, and the executive branch on one side, and senior positions in defense contracting companies and lobbying firms on the other. A retired general becomes a board member of a company whose contracts he previously approved. A Pentagon procurement official becomes a lobbyist for the contractor whose bids he previously evaluated.[11]

In 2018, the Project On Government Oversight documented 380 instances of this pattern over a decade: 380 senior government officials, flag officers, and senior executives who had moved through the revolving door between the Pentagon and the defense industry. In some cases, retired officers were lobbying their former colleagues within days of leaving government service. The legal waiting period supposed to prevent this is routinely gamed or simply ignored.

The Most Prominent Examples

Dick Cheney served as Secretary of Defense under President George H.W. Bush, then became CEO of Halliburton, collecting compensation placed in a range from roughly $34 million to over $44 million. He then became Vice President and oversaw the invasion of Iraq. During his vice presidency, Halliburton's subsidiary KBR received no-bid contracts in Iraq worth billions of dollars. Cheney maintained his financial ties to Halliburton were severed — a claim his own financial disclosure forms, which showed continued deferred compensation payments throughout his vice presidency, directly complicated.[12]

Lloyd Austin served as a four-star general before retiring in 2016, then joined the board of Raytheon Technologies. In 2021, President Biden nominated him as Secretary of Defense. Because the law requires a seven-year waiting period before retired military officers can serve in that role, Austin required a congressional waiver. The Senate granted it. Austin returned to overseeing Pentagon budgets and contracts that directly affected the company from whose board he had just resigned.[13]

James Mattis followed an almost identical path — four-star general, General Dynamics board, then Secretary of Defense under Trump, requiring the same congressional waiver. Mark Esper, who succeeded Mattis, had served as the chief Washington lobbyist for Raytheon before becoming Secretary of the Army and then Secretary of Defense. He disclosed this openly at his confirmation hearing. The Senate confirmed him anyway.[14][15]

These are not isolated cases. They are the norm. The individuals who decide which weapons systems to build, which contractors to fund, and which wars to fight are, with remarkable regularity, the same individuals who previously worked for or subsequently work for the companies that build the weapons, receive the funds, and profit from the wars.

Part Four

The Money

Who Funds the War Caucus

The defense industry's influence over Congress is not left to chance. It is purchased systematically through campaign contributions, lobbying expenditures, and the strategic placement of defense industry employment in as many congressional districts as possible. By ensuring that major defense programs create jobs in dozens of states, the industry builds a congressional coalition for its own funding that transcends party lines. A senator who votes to cut the F-35 program is not just voting against Lockheed Martin. He is voting against jobs in his own state.[16]

OpenSecrets.org has documented the flow of defense industry contributions to congressional candidates for decades. The pattern is consistent. The largest recipients of defense industry money are not members of one party — they are members of the Armed Services Committees and the Appropriations Committees in both chambers: the precise committees that control defense spending and oversight. The industry does not buy ideology. It buys access and control over the specific legislative nodes that govern its revenue.[17]

$900B

The U.S. defense budget passed with overwhelming bipartisan support in a recent year — the same cycle the defense industry spent over $100 million on lobbying and campaign contributions combined.

Part Five

The Lies

Iraq and the Weapons That Were Not There

The selling of the Iraq War is the most thoroughly documented case of deliberate government deception in recent American history — and it was documented largely by the government itself. The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, in its 2004 report, concluded that the key assessments used to justify the invasion were not supported by the underlying intelligence, and that analysts had been pressured to conform their conclusions to a policy that had already been decided.[18]

The Downing Street Memo, a classified British government document leaked in 2005, recorded a meeting of senior British officials in July 2002 — nine months before the invasion. The head of MI6, reporting on a recent visit to Washington, told Prime Minister Blair's cabinet that in Washington, "the intelligence and facts were being fixed around the policy." This was not an allegation by a critic. It was the assessment of a senior allied intelligence chief, in a classified government document, written eight months before the first bomb fell on Baghdad.[19]

After the invasion, the Iraq Survey Group produced its final report in September 2004. It found no active WMD programs. It found no significant production capability since the early 1990s. The weapons presented to the world as certainty — as "a slam dunk" in CIA Director George Tenet's famous phrase — did not exist.[20] Colin Powell, who had delivered the administration's case to the UN Security Council, eventually called it "a blot" on his record.[21]

The Gulf of Tonkin: The Precedent

Iraq was not the first time. In August 1964, the Johnson administration reported that North Vietnamese torpedo boats had twice attacked American destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, giving the President sweeping authority to escalate in Vietnam — the legal foundation for a war that would kill 58,000 Americans and several million Vietnamese.[22]

A declassified NSA study, completed by the agency's own historian and suppressed for decades before being released in 2005, concluded that the second attack — the one that triggered the resolution — had not occurred. NSA signals intelligence showed that midlevel officers had allowed intelligence reports to be altered to make the second attack appear real. The war that consumed a generation of Americans was authorized on the basis of an attack that the government's own records show did not happen.[23]

Iran and the Nuclear Threat That Never Materialized

For more than two decades, American political figures and media commentators have regularly declared that Iran is on the verge of acquiring a nuclear weapon. In November 2007, the Director of National Intelligence released a National Intelligence Estimate representing the consensus of sixteen American intelligence agencies. It concluded with high confidence that Iran had halted its nuclear weapons program in 2003 and had not restarted it.[24] The International Atomic Energy Agency, which has conducted continuous inspections of Iranian nuclear facilities, has never confirmed that Iran possesses a nuclear weapon. The claim of Iranian nuclear imminence has been made repeatedly for over twenty years, repeatedly contradicted by available intelligence, and repeatedly recycled anyway.

Libya and the Humanitarian Lie

In March 2011, the United States and NATO intervened in Libya ostensibly to protect civilians from an imminent massacre. The British Parliament subsequently conducted an inquiry. Its September 2016 report concluded that the threat to civilians had been "vastly overstated," that the intervention had been based on "erroneous assumptions," and that it transformed a limited civil conflict into a prolonged civil war that destabilized the entire region. The country NATO bombed into regime change has had no functioning central government since.[25]

Part Six

The Megaphone

How the Media Sells the Wars

The Cheney–New York Times loop described in the introduction was not a one-time event. Between September 2002 and March 2003, Times reporter Judith Miller published a series of front-page stories about Iraqi weapons of mass destruction, based primarily on anonymous intelligence sources and Iraqi defectors. The stories were alarming, detailed, and authoritative in tone. They were also, in most of their essential claims, wrong.[26] The Times' own editors eventually published a partial mea culpa in May 2004, acknowledging that a number of its pre-war stories had been insufficiently skeptical.[27]

The Pentagon's Television Generals

The Judith Miller case is widely known. The case documented by Times reporter David Barstow in his 2008 Pulitzer Prize-winning investigation is less widely known, and in some ways more disturbing. Barstow obtained, through a Freedom of Information Act request, Pentagon documents revealing a systematic program in which the Defense Department recruited and managed a network of retired military officers who appeared on television news programs as independent military analysts.[28]

The analysts were given exclusive briefings by senior Pentagon officials and coordination on talking points. They were not required to disclose any of this to the networks that employed them. The majority had undisclosed financial relationships with defense contractors — a direct financial incentive to support the wars and the weapons programs that sustained their income. The viewer watching a retired general on CNN explain why the war was going well had no way of knowing that general was simultaneously collecting a paycheck from a company with a billion-dollar Pentagon contract.

Who Owns the Microphone

The structural reason the media fails on war is not only individual corruption. It is ownership. The consolidation of American media into a small number of large conglomerates has placed major news organizations under the control of corporations with significant financial stakes in the defense industry. The most striking example is General Electric, which was simultaneously one of the largest defense contractors in the United States and the parent company of NBC News. The incentive to scrutinize the military-industrial complex was, structurally, absent.[29]

The result is a media environment in which the range of acceptable debate about war runs from "we should do this" to "we should do this differently." The idea that the United States should not be perpetually at war — that the entire enterprise is driven by financial and political interests rather than national security — is simply not a position that receives serious airtime on the major networks. It is not that the evidence for this position is lacking. It is that the position falls outside what media scholar Daniel Hallin called the "sphere of legitimate controversy."[30]

Part Seven

The Hidden Driver

The Petrodollar System

There is one structural incentive for American military involvement in the Middle East that is almost never discussed in mainstream political debate. Since 1973, by agreement between the United States and Saudi Arabia, global oil has been traded in American dollars. This arrangement — the petrodollar system — means that any nation wanting to buy oil must first acquire dollars, creating a permanent global demand for American currency that allows the United States to run budget and trade deficits that would otherwise be unsustainable.[31]

Any leader who moves to sell oil in a currency other than the dollar represents a structural threat to the foundational mechanism that sustains American economic power. The pattern is worth examining: Saddam Hussein announced in 2000 that Iraq would begin selling its oil in euros. He was overthrown three years later; Iraqi oil sales reverted to dollar denomination. Muammar Gaddafi proposed the creation of a gold-backed African currency for oil transactions. Declassified State Department emails document, among the motivations discussed for the Libyan intervention, explicit concern about Gaddafi's gold dinar proposal. Gaddafi was overthrown and killed.[32]

This is not to argue that the petrodollar system was the sole motivation for either war. It is to say that the pattern — challenge the dollar, face regime change — has repeated itself with enough consistency that the absence of any official acknowledgment is itself worth noting.

Part Eight

The Legal Architecture of Permanent War

On September 14, 2001, three days after the attacks on the World Trade Center and the Pentagon, Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force. The document is a single page long. It authorizes the President to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks of September 11. It has no expiration date. It names no specific enemy. It imposes no geographic limit.[33]

That one page of legislation has since been used to justify military operations in at least nineteen countries. Congress, which under the Constitution holds the exclusive power to declare war, has effectively delegated that power to the executive branch in perpetuity, through a document it passed in three days without serious debate.[34]

The one member of Congress who voted against it was Representative Barbara Lee of California. She delivered a brief statement explaining that she could not in good conscience vote for a blank check for war. She received death threats. She was called a traitor. The final vote was 420 to 1.[35]

Consecutive years the National Defense Authorization Act has passed — through wars, recessions, government shutdowns, and political crises that paralyzed every other function of the federal government. On war funding, Congress does not shut down.

Part Nine

The Theater

The False Left–Right Divide

American political life is presented as a war of irreconcilable opposites. Democrats and Republicans fight over abortion, gun control, immigration, and the entire landscape of the culture wars with genuine ferocity. Cable news has built an entire industry on the premise that the country is divided into two hostile tribes that agree on nothing. And then there is war. On war, the votes tell a different story. The 2001 AUMF passed 420 to 1 in the House and 98 to 0 in the Senate. The NDAA passes every year with large majorities of both parties. The largest defense budgets in American history have been passed by both Republican and Democratic congresses, signed by both Republican and Democratic presidents.[37]

The disagreement between the two parties on war is almost entirely theatrical. It is about framing and credit and blame, not about the fundamental question of whether the United States should maintain a global empire sustained by permanent military force. That question is not asked in mainstream political debate, because the financial interests that fund both parties depend on the answer remaining yes.

The Honest Dissenters

There have always been honest dissenters, and the treatment they receive is itself a form of evidence. Ron Paul of Texas served ten terms in the House as a Republican, voting against the Iraq War authorization, the Afghanistan AUMF, and every defense budget he considered unconstitutional. He was an anti-imperialist in the tradition of the Founders, arguing that the Constitution did not authorize the kind of global military empire the United States had built. On July 10, 2003, he delivered a floor speech titled "Neo-Conned" to what was largely an empty House chamber. It was entered into the Congressional Record and largely ignored by the press.[38][39]

In May 2007, during a Republican primary debate, Paul explained that the September 11 attacks were in part a product of blowback — the unintended consequences of decades of American military intervention in the Middle East — a position documented in the 9/11 Commission Report itself. Rudy Giuliani interrupted to demand a retraction. Paul declined. He was right, and the evidence was in the government's own official account of the attacks. Being right was less important than being acceptable to the establishment consensus.[40]

In March 2013, Rand Paul conducted a 13-hour Senate filibuster to force the Obama administration to answer whether it claimed authority to use lethal drone strikes against American citizens on American soil without due process. The administration's initial position was that it did claim that authority. Paul's filibuster forced a clarification.[41]

Bernie Sanders, from the opposite end of the political spectrum, voted against the Iraq War authorization in 2002, repeatedly invoked the War Powers Act to force votes on Yemen, and has consistently pointed out that the United States spends more on defense than the next ten nations combined while its own infrastructure crumbles and its citizens cannot afford healthcare.[42] Paul and Sanders agree on almost nothing else. Their agreement on this single question — that the permanent war machine is incompatible with a functioning democracy — is itself the most powerful argument that the anti-war position is not ideological. It is the position the evidence supports. The establishment has been wrong about every war.

Part Ten

The Bill

What the Wars Have Actually Produced

The Brown University Costs of War Project has estimated that total post-9/11 war spending, including future obligations for veteran care, will exceed $8 trillion. That figure is not the defense budget. It is the cost specifically attributable to the wars in Afghanistan, Iraq, Syria, and related operations since September 11, 2001.[43] Joseph Stiglitz and Linda Bilmes published their analysis of the Iraq War's cost in 2008, estimating three trillion dollars. At the time the figure was disputed by the administration. It has since proven conservative.[44]

$8T

Estimated total cost of post-9/11 American wars, including future veteran care obligations — per the Brown University Costs of War Project, the most rigorous independent accounting available.

The Veterans

The Department of Veterans Affairs documents approximately 6,000 veteran suicides per year — a rate substantially higher than the age-adjusted rate for non-veteran adults. More American veterans of the post-9/11 wars have died by suicide than were killed in combat in those wars.[45] A RAND Corporation study estimated that approximately 20 percent of veterans who served in Iraq and Afghanistan suffered from post-traumatic stress disorder or major depression, and found the mental health care system dramatically underfunded relative to the need.[46]

The World Left Behind

The Costs of War Project estimates over 900,000 people killed directly from violence in post-9/11 wars, and approximately 38 million people displaced from their homes — comparable to the largest refugee crises of the twentieth century. The countries that were invaded to make America safer are, without exception, less stable, less safe, and less functional than they were before the invasions. Afghanistan fell to the Taliban within days of the American withdrawal. Iraq has a sectarian government beholden to Iran. Libya has no government at all.[47]

The Country Left Behind

While the United States spent trillions fighting wars abroad, the American Society of Civil Engineers graded American infrastructure a C-minus — crumbling roads, failing bridges, aging water systems, deteriorating public transit.[48] The National Priorities Project has calculated what the money spent on post-9/11 wars could have funded domestically: universal pre-K for decades, free public college tuition, a complete modernization of the national electrical grid, and full funding of the VA healthcare system for generations.[49]

This is the opportunity cost of permanent war. The country that cannot fix its water pipes is spending $900 billion a year on its military. The country where military veterans are killing themselves at a rate of sixteen per day spent twenty years and two trillion dollars trying to build a democracy in Afghanistan, and then watched it collapse in seventy-two hours.

Conclusion

The Machine and Its Mechanics

The permanent war machine is not a conspiracy. It does not require secret meetings or shadowy coordination. It requires only that each of its components follow its own institutional interests, and that the sum of those interests adds up to war. The defense contractor follows its interest in revenue. The politician follows her interest in campaign contributions and constituent employment. The lobbyist follows his interest in fees. The retired general follows his interest in board compensation. The think tank follows its interest in foundation grants from defense-connected donors. The television network follows its interest in access to government officials and advertising revenue from corporations with defense contracts. Each of them, individually, is simply doing what their incentives demand. Together, they constitute a system in which war is the default, peace is the aberration, and the question of whether any given conflict actually serves American interests is almost never seriously asked.

Ron Paul asked it, from the House floor, for thirty years. Rand Paul asks it from the Senate floor today. Bernie Sanders asks it. Barbara Lee asked it on September 14, 2001, alone among 535 members of Congress. They have been mocked, marginalized, stripped of committee assignments, called unserious, called dangerous, called traitors. And they have been right. The weapons of mass destruction were not there. The connections to September 11 were fabricated. The mission was never accomplished. The wars did not end when the troops came home. The Taliban is back in Kabul. The bill is $8 trillion and counting. The veterans are dying.

Eisenhower told us this would happen in 1961. The documents have confirmed it. The contractors' SEC filings prove it. The congressional voting records show it. The only question that remains is whether the American public will ever demand an accounting from the machine that has been running their country, and their money, and their children's lives, for the past sixty years.
